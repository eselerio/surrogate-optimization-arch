\begin{abstract}
Operational optimization of activated sludge plants requires repeated evaluation of interacting biological, recycle, and settling processes. This study develops a plant-wide extension of invariant-constrained second-order regression and compares surrogate-based optimization with direct smooth mechanistic optimization. The objective is to assess whether physical reconciliation can support useful operating decisions while reducing repeated optimization cost. The surrogate predicts mixer and reactor concentrations, clarifier outlet component flows, and aggregate clarifier solids inventory. A constrained projection reconciles these predictions with recycle mixing, conserved inventories, clarifier balances, outlet-derived inventory bounds, and non-negativity. A separate log-scale regression supplies a positive overflow solids concentration. The case study uses five equal-volume reactors, independent aeration in reactors 3--5, a ten-layer mechanistic clarifier, and 10,000 mechanistically simulated plant states. Model fitting uses 8,000 states and plant-grouped five-fold cross-validation. The remaining 2,000 states and ten influent scenarios provide descriptive post-selection evidence. Numerical audits distinguish first-order stationarity from finite-resolution convergence evidence. Selected decisions are assessed on the same nonsmooth mechanistic reference. The paired comparison does not use a minimum solids-age eligibility threshold, although the selected decisions were obtained with an 8-d lower bound. The comparison separates prediction accuracy, physical consistency, decision quality, and computational cost. It does not assume that enforcing selected physical constraints reproduces the full mechanistic model or establishes global optimality.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\section{Introduction}

Activated sludge treatment couples biological reactors with secondary clarification and sludge recirculation. Return activated sludge (RAS) carries settled biomass back to the reactors, whereas waste activated sludge (WAS) removes solids from the plant. Plants designed for biological nitrogen removal may also use mixed-liquor recycle (MLR) to return reactor liquor to upstream stages \citep{Henze2008}. Clarifier settling and thickening affect the solids returned to the reactors and the quality of the treated water \citep{HartelPopel1992}. Sludge age also influences oxygen-transfer efficiency, effluent particle concentrations, and removal of biodegradable organic matter \citep{Leu2012}. These coupled effects link recycle, aeration, and wasting decisions. Mathematical programming provides a framework for balancing treatment requirements, resource use, and constraints in wastewater treatment design \citep{PadronPaez2020,Aboagye2021}. Operational optimization applies this framework to the choice of plant settings.

Activated Sludge Models describe the biological and chemical processes through coupled nonlinear dynamic mass-balance equations \citep{Henze2006}. Settling models describe clarification and thickening \citep{Takacs1991}. Time integration can approach a steady state and provide an initial state for a subsequent algebraic calculation \citep{KelleyKeyes1998,Selerio2026}. In an activated sludge application, \citet{Selerio2026} used dynamic relaxation to improve this initial state when the algebraic solve did not converge. When optimization evaluates candidate operating conditions through simulation, the steady-state calculation must be repeated. This repeated evaluation motivates the use of less expensive surrogate models \citep{Bhosekar2018}.

Statistical surrogate modeling offers an alternative to repeatedly solving the mechanistic equations during optimization. Surrogates are fitted to input--output data from experiments, mechanistic simulations, or a combination of these sources \citep{McBride2019}. The fitted model predicts process responses at new inputs and can reduce the cost of repeated feasibility and optimization calculations \citep{Bhosekar2018}.

Statistical surrogates can reduce the cost of repeated process evaluation. For papermaking wastewater treatment, \citet{He2023} used a Kriging surrogate to support optimization of treatment performance and greenhouse gas emissions at lower computational cost than mechanistic simulation. Faster evaluation does not ensure physically meaningful predictions. Unconstrained activated sludge surrogates can predict negative component concentrations or violate stoichiometric mass conservation \citep{Selerio2026}.

Effluent prediction also requires attention to low residual concentrations. Longer sludge age can improve removal of biodegradable organic matter and reduce effluent particles and suspended solids \citep{Leu2012}. For a component with a low residual concentration, a small absolute prediction error can be a large relative error. For example, an error of 1 g m$^{-3}$ is only 1\% of an influent concentration of 100 g m$^{-3}$. The same error is 100\% of a treated-water concentration of 1 g m$^{-3}$. Since concentrations cannot be negative, overprediction of removal can also produce a physically impossible result. The activated sludge comparison of \citet{Selerio2026} shows that favorable aggregate prediction errors can coexist with violations of conservation and non-negativity. Prediction accuracy and physical consistency must therefore be assessed separately. This distinction matters during optimization because the search for favorable surrogate predictions can exploit model errors \citep{Bhosekar2018}.

These risks motivate the explicit enforcement of physical constraints on statistical surrogate predictions. The purpose is to restrict predictions to states that satisfy known process requirements, rather than to assume that lower average error ensures physical consistency. Activated sludge and wastewater studies have addressed parts of this problem through hybrid models and constrained uses of surrogates. \citet{Li2024} embedded governing-equation residuals in physics-informed neural networks for unit-operation models, including an activated sludge reactor, and reported lower data requirements and better generalization across loading conditions than conventional machine learning models. At the operational level, \citet{Bernardelli2020} placed a neuro-fuzzy process model inside real-time model predictive control to select aeration while jointly pursuing nutrient-removal and energy objectives. At the broader wastewater-process level, \citet{Durkin2024} used data-driven unit surrogates within constrained superstructure optimization for resource recovery.

These approaches differ in which constraints they impose and where they impose them. A penalty on equation residuals encourages physical agreement but does not enforce exact conservation by construction \citep{Beucler2021}. Bounds on operating decisions likewise do not impose a conservation relation on surrogate outputs unless that relation is explicitly enforced. In activated sludge modeling, \citet{Selerio2026} projected second-order regression predictions onto mass-conservation and component-nonnegativity constraints. This approach retains a statistical input--output model while ensuring that each projected prediction satisfies the enforced constraints to numerical tolerance.

Related studies outside activated sludge modeling show that physical constraints can be introduced at different stages of surrogate construction. Analytic conservation constraints have been incorporated into neural network output mappings for atmospheric convection \citep{Beucler2021}. For atmospheric photochemistry, \citet{SturmWexler2020} formulated conserved fluxes as learning targets. \citet{SturmWexler2022} later embedded the stoichiometric mapping as a fixed layer that conserves atoms to numerical precision. Other studies have used constrained training data and a mass-balance penalty to reduce conservation errors \citep{Ruckstuhl2021}. Physical consistency can also be imposed after an unconstrained model has made a prediction. \citet{SturmSilva2025} applied a species-weighted correction that enforces atom conservation. \citet{Valente2025} projected surrogate outputs onto manifolds defined by conservation laws. These applications concern atmospheric and plasma systems. The activated sludge projection of \citet{Selerio2026} uses the same principle of correcting predictions to satisfy known balances.

The cited studies address complementary parts of the plant-wide problem. The Invariant-Constrained Second-Order Regression (ICSOR) formulation of \citet{Selerio2026} applies to the response of a single continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR). It does not represent the mixer, reactor train, secondary clarifier, and recycle streams as one coupled response. In contrast, \citet{He2023} demonstrates surrogate-assisted optimization of a wastewater treatment process, but does not establish hard output-space reconciliation of all connected component responses. The gap addressed here is the integration of these two capabilities. Physical consistency within one CSTR does not establish consistency of recycle mixing or clarifier outlet balances across the plant. The proposed extension must therefore preserve the physical connections among units, enforce engineering limits during the search, and verify selected decisions against a mechanistic reference. Physical feasibility of a surrogate prediction must remain distinct from prediction accuracy and decision quality.

This study develops and evaluates a plant-wide extension of ICSOR to address the computational cost and physical reliability of surrogate-based operational optimization. The objective is to determine whether a physically constrained statistical surrogate can support the selection of operating conditions that balance treatment performance and resource use. The extended model represents the mixer, reactor train, clarifier, and recycle streams jointly and reconciles their predictions with plant-wide physical constraints. Three research questions guide the evaluation. First, how does this reconciliation affect physical consistency and prediction accuracy across the plant? Second, do the surrogate-selected operating conditions satisfy the engineering limits and retain their predicted performance when evaluated with the mechanistic model? Third, how do decision quality and computational cost compare with direct mechanistic optimization under common operating choices, objective priorities, and reference-based engineering assessment? Separate prediction assessment and mechanistic evaluation of the selected decisions address these questions. The assessment remains descriptive because the final analysis protocol was not specified independently of all inspected data. Satisfaction of the imposed physical constraints is therefore assessed separately from prediction accuracy and decision quality.

\section{Theory and calculation}

The modeling problem is to select reactor capacity, aeration, recycle, and wasting settings for a coupled activated sludge plant at steady state. Each choice affects both local unit conditions and the material returned through the recycle streams. The formulation therefore begins with hydraulic and component balances that connect the mixer, reactors, and clarifier. These relations define the physical requirements used to reconcile the statistical predictions. The resulting plant response supplies the treatment and resource measures used in the objective and engineering limits. The section then develops the surrogate optimization problem and a direct mechanistic comparator. Numerical solution, route-specific normalization, and retrospective verification are described in the methodology. Both routes are judged on the same mechanistic reference without assuming that physical reconciliation reproduces the full model.

\subsection{Plant configuration and hydraulic balances}

The hydraulic balances establish how each recycle setting changes the flow through the treatment units. Consider $N\ge1$ completely mixed biological stages followed by an $L\ge3$ layer clarifier. The component representation distinguishes soluble transport from particulate settling and thickening \citep{Takacs1991,JeppssonDiehl1996}. Component vectors contain $F=F_S+F_X$ entries. The matrices $E_S\in\R^{F_S\times F}$ and $E_X\in\R^{F_X\times F}$ select the disjoint soluble and particulate entries, so $E_S^\top E_S+E_X^\top E_X=I_F$ and $E_SE_X^\top=0$. The non-negative total suspended solids (TSS) map $t_X$ has zero soluble entries. Figure~\ref{fig:flowsheet} shows the plant connections. The final reactor supplies both the MLR stream and the clarifier. Clarifier underflow is split into RAS, which returns internally, and WAS, which leaves the plant.

Normalizing every flow by the fresh-influent flow $Q_0$ gives
\begin{equation}
q_P=1+r_I+r_R,\qquad q_C=1+r_R,\qquad
q_U=r_R+w,\qquad q_E=1-w.
\label{eq:flows}
\end{equation}
The clarifier identity $q_C=q_E+q_U$ follows immediately. The operating domain requires $r_I\ge0$, $r_R\ge0$, $0\le w<1$, and $q_U=r_R+w>0$, so both outlet concentrations can later be recovered safely from their mass flows. At the mixer, fresh feed, MLR, and RAS meet. Dividing its component balance by $Q_0$ gives
\begin{equation}
q_Pm=x+r_Ic_N+r_Rc_U.
\label{eq:mixer}
\end{equation}

Recycle changes the flow through a reactor without changing its volume. The nominal hydraulic retention time (HRT) based on fresh flow must therefore be distinguished from the actual stage residence time. Define
\begin{equation}
h_i=\frac{24V_i}{Q_0},\qquad H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_i,\quad
d_i=\frac{Q_0q_P}{V_i}=\frac{24q_P}{h_i},
\label{eq:stagehydraulics}
\end{equation}
where $h_i$ and $H$ are in hours and $d_i$ is in d$^{-1}$. The actual stage residence time is $h_i/q_P$ hours. Increasing recycle at fixed volume therefore increases dilution and reduces the time spent in each pass through a reactor. Individual stage volumes may be varied through $h_i$, or fixed volume fractions may be imposed through $h_i=f_i^V H$ with $\sum_i f_i^V=1$. Equal tanks correspond to $f_i^V=1/N$.

The optimization variables must correspond to the choices available in the study. Let $\vartheta\in\R^{d_\vartheta}$ contain only these freely varied quantities. They may include selected $h_i$, aeration settings $a_i$, stage-addition rates, and the three recycle and wasting ratios. A stage addition represented only by a source term is assumed to have negligible carrier flow. If its liquid flow is not negligible relative to $Q_0$, that stream must also enter the hydraulic balances and change the downstream flow and dilution rates. Fixed settings remain parameters rather than zero-variance regression inputs. This definition avoids fitting coefficients for settings that never vary.

\subsection{Reactor balances and conserved inventories}

The hydraulic relations determine the dilution rate used in each reactor balance. These balances connect the operating settings to biological conversion and provide conservation relations for the surrogate. In reactor $i$, material enters from the preceding stage, leaves at the train flow, and is transformed by biological and chemical processes. Oxygen transfer and declared external additions provide separate sources. With $c_0=m$, the steady component balance is
\begin{equation}
0=d_i(c_{i-1}-c_i)+\nu^\top\rho_i(c_i;\vartheta)
+\omega_i(a_i,c_i)e_{S_O}+\xi_i(\vartheta),
\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.
\label{eq:cstr}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu\in\R^{n_p\times F}$ stores the stoichiometric changes caused by $n_p$ processes, and $\rho_i\in\R_+^{n_p}$ contains their volumetric rates. The vector $e_{S_O}$ selects dissolved oxygen (DO), and $\omega_i=k_La_i(a_i)(S_O^*-c_{i,S_O})$ describes oxygen transfer. The term $\xi_i$ represents an explicitly modeled stage addition with negligible carrier flow. Every term has units of component concentration per day. Retaining $\xi_i$ ensures that an optimized carbon or chemical dose enters the material balance. At a zero component concentration, the combined reaction and source terms are required to be non-negative. For a locally Lipschitz dynamic balance with non-negative initial conditions and inflows, this boundary condition preserves non-negative concentrations \citep{Haddad2010}. This property of the continuous model does not guarantee non-negative numerical integration steps.

The surrogate should preserve conserved inventories without solving every reaction-rate equation during prediction. Some linear combinations of components are changed neither by the reaction network nor by oxygen transfer. Let $A\in\R^{K\times F}$ contain independent, physically identified inventory rows satisfying
\begin{equation}
A\nu^\top=0,\qquad Ae_{S_O}=0,\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(A)=K,\qquad \|A_{k,:}\|_2=1.
\label{eq:invariants}
\end{equation}
Premultiplication of Equation~\eqref{eq:cstr} by $A$ removes the reaction and aeration terms and gives the stagewise inventory balance
\begin{equation}
A(c_i-c_{i-1})=d_i^{-1}A\xi_i(\vartheta),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.
\label{eq:rails}
\end{equation}
When $\xi_i=0$, the quantity $Ac_i$ is constant across the stage. Reactions can redistribute a conserved inventory among components but cannot create or remove it. These relations prevent the surrogate from improving a predicted treatment response by violating an inventory balance. If a stage dose adds a conserved quantity, Equation~\eqref{eq:rails} accounts for that addition. Its right-hand side can remain zero only when $A\xi_i=0$.

\subsection{Clarifier outlet balances and aggregate inventory}

The final reactor supplies the clarifier feed. The clarifier outlet balances complete the recycle connection to the mixer and determine the component flows leaving the plant. Since $q_U$ may be small, these balances are written in outlet component flows rather than outlet concentrations. Define
\begin{equation}
g_E=q_Ec_E,\qquad g_U=q_Uc_U.
\label{eq:massflows}
\end{equation}
The component balance around the complete vessel is then
\begin{equation}
q_Cc_N=g_E+g_U.
\label{eq:clarifierbalance}
\end{equation}
Because $q_E,q_U>0$, the concentrations are recovered as $c_E=g_E/q_E$ and $c_U=g_U/q_U$. Using flows in the balance avoids dividing by a small underflow ratio within the conservation constraint.

The clarifier is treated here as a nonreactive separator, following the settling-model approach of \citet{Takacs1991}. Under this assumption, soluble material follows the liquid and particulate material can concentrate in the underflow. The linear relations retained in the projection are
\begin{equation}
E_Sg_U=q_UE_Sc_N,\qquad E_Xg_U\ge q_UE_Xc_N.
\label{eq:clarificationrules}
\end{equation}
The first relation, together with Equation~\eqref{eq:clarifierbalance}, also gives $E_Sg_E=q_EE_Sc_N$. Thus the clarifier does not remove soluble material through settling. The second relation prevents an underflow particulate concentration below the feed concentration. With non-negative outlet flows, it bounds the underflow recovery of each particulate component with positive feed concentration between $q_U/q_C$ and one. Applying the non-negative TSS map and the total clarifier balance then gives $X_E\le X_N\le X_U$, where $X_N=t_X^\top c_N$ is the feed TSS concentration.

Outlet concentrations determine the returned and discharged solids, but they do not determine the solids stored inside the clarifier. This stored mass is needed to calculate whole-plant solids age. Let $s_\ell$ be the mechanistic TSS concentration in layer $\ell$, numbered from the water surface ($\ell=1$) to the hopper ($\ell=L$). The endpoint concentrations follow from the outlet component flows as
\begin{equation}
X_E=s_1=\frac{t_X^\top g_E}{q_E},\qquad
X_U=s_L=\frac{t_X^\top g_U}{q_U}.
\label{eq:layerendpoints}
\end{equation}
The mechanistic acceptance envelope
\begin{equation}
X_E\le s_\ell\le X_U,\qquad \ell=2,\ldots,L-1,
\label{eq:layerenvelope}
\end{equation}
keeps every internal layer between the two outlet concentrations. It is intentionally an endpoint envelope, not a claim of layer-by-layer monotonicity. The nonlinear settling-flux balances, which determine the detailed shape, remain part of the mechanistic model. With positive layer volumes, the layer values determine the physical inventory
\begin{equation}
M_{\rm cl}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}V_{{\rm cl},\ell}s_\ell,
\qquad V_{\rm cl}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}V_{{\rm cl},\ell}.
\label{eq:inventory}
\end{equation}
Only this scalar inventory is retained in the statistical response. Eliminating the unneeded internal layer coordinates from Equations~\eqref{eq:layerendpoints}--\eqref{eq:inventory} gives the tight interval
\begin{align}
\underline M_{\rm cl}&=(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},L})X_E+V_{{\rm cl},L}X_U,\notag\\
\overline M_{\rm cl}&=V_{{\rm cl},1}X_E+(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},1})X_U,\notag\\
\underline M_{\rm cl}&\le M_{\rm cl}\le\overline M_{\rm cl}.
\label{eq:inventoryenvelope}
\end{align}
For the ordered endpoints $X_E\le X_U$, the interval is necessary and sufficient for the existence of at least one profile satisfying the endpoint envelope and having inventory $M_{\rm cl}$. Every intermediate inventory can be obtained by assigning a common admissible value to the internal layers. These bounds prevent the predicted stored mass from contradicting the predicted outlet concentrations. They retain useful storage information without requiring a statistical prediction for every layer. An admissible inventory does not establish that any corresponding profile satisfies the nonlinear settling-flux equations. That check remains with the mechanistic model.

\subsection{Raw plant-response regression and overflow solids model}

The preceding balances identify the quantities needed to represent the connected plant without predicting every clarifier layer. The extended ICSOR surrogate, designated route $S$, therefore predicts the mixer and reactor states, outlet component flows, and aggregate inventory together. These quantities supply the objective and constraint calculations developed below. The response vector is ordered in process sequence as
\begin{equation}
\chi=\col(m,c_1,\ldots,c_N,g_E,g_U,M_{\rm cl})
\in\R^{n_\chi},\qquad n_\chi=(N+3)F+1.
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
The endpoint TSS values remain recoverable from $g_E$ and $g_U$, while $M_{\rm cl}$ preserves the storage information required for solids retention time (SRT). The full $L$-layer profile remains in the mechanistic target from which this reduced response is constructed.

The regression includes interactions among influent composition and operating settings. For example, oxygen demand depends on the load entering the reactors \citep{Henze2008}. Centering and scaling put the regression features on comparable scales before ridge regularization \citep{Hastie2009}. Let $z=\col[D_{\vartheta,{\rm fit}}^{-1}(\vartheta-\bar\vartheta),D_{x,{\rm fit}}^{-1}(x-\bar x)]\in\R^{d_z}$, where $d_z=d_\vartheta+F$ and the means and positive diagonal scales are computed from the model-development data. To include curvature and pairwise interactions without duplicate terms, form
\begin{equation}
r(z)=\col[z,\vech(zz^\top)],\qquad
\phi_S(\vartheta,x)=\col[1,D_{r,{\rm fit}}^{-1}\{r(z)-\bar r\}]
\in\R^{n_{\phi,S}},
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
where $\vech$ lists one triangular copy of the symmetric quadratic matrix. Hence
\begin{equation}
n_{\phi,S}=1+d_z+\frac{d_z(d_z+1)}2.
\label{eq:featurecount}
\end{equation}

With response mean $\bar\chi$ and positive diagonal scale matrix $D_\chi$ computed from the model-development set, the route-$S$ raw response is
\begin{equation}
\chi_{{\rm raw},S}(\vartheta,x)=\bar\chi+D_\chi\widehat C_{S,\gamma_S}\phi_S(\vartheta,x).
\label{eq:raw}
\end{equation}
The ridge parameter $\gamma_S$ controls a squared-coefficient penalty that stabilizes estimation when features are correlated \citep{Hastie2009}. Equation~\eqref{eq:raw} is still an unconstrained regression prediction. It does not enforce sign or balance requirements.

Overflow TSS spans a strongly asymmetric range. Small absolute errors at low treated-water concentrations can correspond to large relative errors. A fit over the full plant response may give these errors little weight when other responses have much larger magnitudes. A separate logarithmic model therefore places the endpoint on a relative-error scale without changing the raw multiresponse fit \citep{Tofallis2015}. Define the dimensionless logarithmic training target
\begin{equation}
\ell_E=\log\!\left(\frac{X_E}{X_\star}\right),
\qquad X_\star=1\ {\rm mg\,L^{-1}},
\label{eq:logtarget}
\end{equation}
and fit a separate scalar ridge coefficient vector $\widehat\beta_{E,\gamma_E}$ on the same complete second-order input family. Its positive concentration-scale prediction is
\begin{equation}
\widehat X_{E,\log}(\vartheta,x)
=X_\star\exp\!\left\{\widehat\beta_{E,\gamma_E}^{\top}
\phi_S(\vartheta,x)\right\}>0.
\label{eq:logclosure}
\end{equation}
The logarithm assigns the same discrepancy to a twofold error at low or high concentration. Exponentiation prevents a negative fitted endpoint. The positive prediction varies with all declared controls and influent coordinates and is not a mechanistic lower bound. The fitting procedure is described in the methodology and the Supplementary Material, Section~S3.

\subsection{Joint projection of the plant response}

The two regressions supply a raw plant response and a positive overflow-TSS estimate, but neither enforces the network balances. A joint projection reconciles these predictions with the physical relations derived above. Correcting the complete response together preserves the connections between units and recycle streams. The physical balances remain distinct from the empirical overflow closure. Define $\delta_i(\vartheta)=d_i^{-1}A\xi_i(\vartheta)$ and let $\mathcal H(\vartheta)\chi=\mathfrak b(\vartheta,x)$ represent
\begin{equation}
\col\!\left(
\begin{array}{c}
q_Pm-r_Ic_N-(r_R/q_U)g_U-x\\
\{A(c_i-c_{i-1})-\delta_i\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad c_0=m\\
g_E+g_U-q_Cc_N\\
E_Sg_U-q_UE_Sc_N
\end{array}\right)=0.
\label{eq:Hbase}
\end{equation}
The network balances alone do not determine the degree of solids separation. The fitted overflow endpoint supplies this additional empirical relation through
\begin{equation}
t_X^\top g_E=q_E\widehat X_{E,\log}(\vartheta,x).
\label{eq:overflowclosure}
\end{equation}
At fixed $(\vartheta,x)$, the right-hand side is a known positive scalar, so Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure} is linear in $\chi$ and preserves the quadratic program (QP) structure. The complete equality operator $\mathcal H_{\log}(\vartheta)\chi=\mathfrak b_{\log}(\vartheta,x)$ collects Equations~\eqref{eq:Hbase} and \eqref{eq:overflowclosure}. There are
\begin{equation}
n_h=2F+NK+F_S+1
\label{eq:eqcount}
\end{equation}
rows covering the mixer balance, $N$ invariant transitions, the full clarifier balance, soluble pass-through, and the scalar overflow-TSS closure. The closure fixes only total overflow TSS. It does not prescribe the particulate composition of $g_E$. That allocation is reconciled jointly with the raw component predictions and the remaining constraints.

Particulate densification and inventory bounds restrict responses that could satisfy the equalities but still contradict the adopted settling envelope. These inequalities are written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal G(\vartheta)\chi=
\col\!\left(
\begin{array}{c}
q_UE_Xc_N-E_Xg_U\\
q_U(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},L})t_X^\top g_E
+q_EV_{{\rm cl},L}t_X^\top g_U-q_Eq_UM_{\rm cl}\\
q_Eq_UM_{\rm cl}-q_UV_{{\rm cl},1}t_X^\top g_E
-q_E(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},1})t_X^\top g_U
\end{array}\right)\le0.
\label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
Thus $n_g=F_X+2$ inequality rows describe particulate densification and aggregate-inventory feasibility. The last two rows are the division-free form of Equation~\eqref{eq:inventoryenvelope} and remain linear in $\chi$ at fixed $\vartheta$. Non-negativity contributes another $n_\chi$ inequalities.

The correction is measured after scaling each response by $D_\chi^{-1}$, so that the large-magnitude inventory coordinate does not overwhelm a small nutrient coordinate. The projected displacement and response are
\begin{align}
\widehat u_S(\vartheta,x)=\argminop_{u\in\R^{n_\chi}}\quad&\tfrac12\|u\|_2^2
\label{eq:projection}\\
\text{subject to}\quad&
 D_b^{-1}\{\mathcal H_{\log}(\vartheta)[\chi_{{\rm raw},S}+D_\chi u]-\mathfrak b_{\log}(\vartheta,x)\}=0,\notag\\
&\chi_{{\rm raw},S}+D_\chi u\ge0,\notag\\
&D_g^{-1}\mathcal G(\vartheta)[\chi_{{\rm raw},S}+D_\chi u]\le0,\notag\\[1mm]
\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x)={}&\chi_{{\rm raw},S}(\vartheta,x)+D_\chi\widehat u_S(\vartheta,x).
\label{eq:projected}
\end{align}
$D_b$ and $D_g$ rescale the constraint rows without changing the feasible set. This scaling improves numerical conditioning \citep[Section~11.8.2]{Boyd2004}. The overflow-closure row and the other equality rows receive positive scales computed from the model-development set. At fixed inputs, the linear constraints define a closed convex set. If this set is nonempty, the strictly convex, coercive objective has a unique minimizer \citep{Boyd2004}. Because Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure} is fitted rather than a physical identity, feasibility is not guaranteed throughout the operating box. It is checked for every grouped out-of-fold model-development row, optimization evaluation, and reported holdout or selected-decision evaluation. An infeasible closure is recorded as a failed projection and is not clipped or relaxed.

Enforcing constraints does not by itself establish greater prediction accuracy. Euclidean projection onto a nonempty closed convex set cannot increase the distance to a reference in that set \citep{Boyd2004}. Here, the distance is measured in the standardized response coordinates used in Equation~\eqref{eq:projection}. However, the mechanistic overflow TSS generally differs from the fitted value in Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure}, so that guarantee is not presumed here. The error audit therefore accounts for the reference's distance to the set, as detailed in the Supplementary Material, Section~S4.2. The projection does not reconstruct a clarifier profile or enforce all kinetic and nonlinear settling equations. Those equations are checked separately by full mechanistic replay.

\subsection{Engineering objective and operating constraints}

The reconciled response provides the concentrations, outlet flows, and stored solids needed to evaluate operating decisions. Effluent-quality targets influence reactor sizing, oxygen supply, recycle, and solids management \citep{Henze2008}. The objective combines treatment and resource measures to represent these competing requirements. Their relative priorities are case-study choices rather than universal constants. Let $z_E=I_{\rm comp}c_E\in\R_+^4$ contain effluent chemical oxygen demand (COD), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and TSS. With positive normalization $D_T$ and quality priorities $\varpi_Q\ge0$, $\mathbf1^\top\varpi_Q=1$, define
\begin{equation}
j_Q=\varpi_Q^\top D_T^{-1}z_E.
\label{eq:qualitycomponent}
\end{equation}
Representative dimensionless resource terms are
\begin{align}
j_H&=\frac{H-H^L}{H^U-H^L},&
j_A&=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}V_i k_La_i}
{E_A^{\rm ref}},\notag\\
j_I&=\frac{r_I-r_I^L}{r_I^U-r_I^L},&
j_R&=\frac{r_R-r_R^L}{r_R^U-r_R^L},&
j_W&=\frac{w\,t_X^\top c_U}{w^UX_U^{\rm ref}}.
\label{eq:objectivecomponents}
\end{align}
$\mathcal A$ is the set of stages whose aeration can be nonzero. The fixed normalization $E_A^{\rm ref}>0$ is chosen before optimization. In the case study, it is the maximum value of $\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}V_i k_La_i$ over the declared operating box. The aeration term therefore measures oxygen-transfer capacity at the selected volumes and aeration settings. It does not represent the actual oxygen-transfer rate, which also depends on the DO deficit. Its denominator does not change when stage volumes are varied. This prevents an increase in capacity from being offset merely by rescaling the objective. These resource terms are engineering proxies rather than a monetary cost model. Additional declared operating terms can be included in the same manner. With $j=\col(j_Q,j_H,j_A,j_I,j_R,j_W)$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
J(\vartheta,\chi;\varpi)=\varpi^\top j(\vartheta,\chi),
\qquad \varpi\ge0,\quad \mathbf1^\top\varpi=1.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The vectors $\varpi$ and $\varpi_Q$ distinguish overall operating priorities from priorities among effluent-quality measures. Their values are stated and justified in the case study. During optimization, $D_T$ denotes the fixed normalization of the selected route. Both exact-reference objectives instead use the direct-route normalization. The case-study definition states the implemented scales explicitly.

An objective alone could favor settings that exceed the plant's operating limits. Engineering constraints therefore restrict solids storage, clarifier loading, and outlet conditions using the same response as the objective. Define $X_i=t_X^\top c_i$ and use $X_E$ and $X_U$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:layerendpoints}. The external solids loss rate is
\begin{equation}
\dot M_X=Q_0(q_EX_E+wX_U).
\end{equation}
The whole-plant SRT, surface overflow rate (SOR), and clarifier solids loading rate (SLR) are then
\begin{align}
\mathrm{SRT}&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_iX_i+M_{\rm cl}}{\dot M_X},
\qquad \dot M_X\ge\epsilon_X>0,\label{eq:srt}\\
\mathrm{SOR}&=\frac{Q_0q_E}{A_{\rm cl}},\label{eq:sor}\\
\mathrm{SLR}&=\frac{10^{-3}Q_0q_CX_N}{A_{\rm cl}}.
\label{eq:slr}
\end{align}
The factor $10^{-3}$ converts grams to kilograms in SLR. Including $M_{\rm cl}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:srt} accounts for solids stored outside the biological reactors. Omitting this inventory would underestimate whole-plant SRT for the same solids loss rate. Upper limits on SRT, SLR, and $X_U$, the feed-solids lower bound $X_N\ge X_F^{\min}>0$, and the positive external-solids-loss guard form $g_{\rm eng}(\vartheta,\chi)\le0$. SOR and effluent limits may be included when required by the case study. A case-specific minimum SRT may also restrict the optimization search. The retained limits and their values are specified in the methodology. The SRT bounds are imposed in cross-multiplied form together with the positive-loss guard to avoid division by a small quantity. Finite SRT remains a descriptive operating metric during exact replay.

Network feasibility does not prevent an optimizer from selecting a response that needs an unusually large correction or is inconsistent with reaction kinetics. The additional constraints $g_{{\rm trust},S}$ restrict such behavior using four diagnostics calibrated on the model-development set. The standardized correction $\kappa_{{\rm corr},S}=\|\widehat u_S\|_2/\sqrt{n_\chi}$ measures how far projection moves the raw response. Feature leverage is commonly used to determine whether a prediction lies within the fitted input domain \citep{Sahigara2012}. The regularized form used here measures support from the fitted input features. Deviation from a common particulate recovery tests consistency with the mechanistic outlet-composition assumption. The scaled smooth reactor-balance residual measures disagreement with reactor kinetics. These diagnostics provide screening rather than a guarantee of mechanistic accuracy. Their formulas and calibration rules are given in the Supplementary Material, Section~S4.3. Correction, recovery, and reactor limits use out-of-fold 95th percentiles. The leverage limit uses the maximum value among model-development rows in the final fit. Neither calculation uses holdout or optimization responses. Nonlinear clarifier behavior is checked only through exact $L$-layer replay because the aggregate response cannot supply layer-flux residuals.

The extended-ICSOR operational problem combines these requirements as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\vartheta}\quad&J[\vartheta,\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x);\varpi]\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\vartheta\in\mathcal V,\\
&g_{\rm eng}[\vartheta,\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x)]\le0,\\
&g_{{\rm trust},S}[\vartheta,\chi_{{\rm raw},S},\widehat\chi_S]\le0,\\
&\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x)\text{ solves Equation~\eqref{eq:projection}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bilevel}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal V$ contains the declared bounds and any stage-allocation equalities. The projected response is unique whenever the lower problem is feasible. The upper problem is generally nonconvex and piecewise smooth because the projection QP can change its active set \citep{Tondel2003,Colson2007}. Accordingly, $\vartheta_S$ denotes the best feasible candidate retained by the search, together with its reported convergence tier. It is not a certified global minimizer.

\subsection{Direct smooth mechanistic comparator}

The surrogate route can now be compared with an optimization model that imposes the mechanistic balances directly. Route $M$ uses the same operating controls and objective components, but replaces regression and projection with reactor and clarifier equations. The methodology distinguishes native normalization and the engineering rules applied to the retained decisions. Its state contains the concentrations that accumulate in the reduced mechanistic model as
\begin{equation}
y=\col(c_1,\ldots,c_N,s_1,\ldots,s_L)\in\R_+^{n_y},
\qquad n_y=NF+L.
\label{eq:directstate}
\end{equation}
The mixer and clarifier outlet vectors are reconstructed algebraically from $(\vartheta,x,y)$. The objective and constraints use the reduced response with $M_{\rm cl}=\sum_{\ell=1}^L V_{{\rm cl},\ell}s_\ell$. This mapping allows both routes to evaluate the same engineering quantities despite their different state dimensions.

Outlet reconstruction divides by the clarifier-feed TSS, which could otherwise reach zero at an infeasible solver iterate. An auxiliary variable $\widetilde X_F\ge X_F^{\min}>0$ supplies a positive denominator, with the equality $\widetilde X_F=t_X^\top c_N$ enforced at feasibility. A differentiable residual $f_{\rm sm}(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F)$ contains all $NF$ reactor balances and all $L$ finite-volume clarifier balances. The direct route also retains the full layer envelope underlying the surrogate inventory bounds. Define $g_{\rm env}(y)=\col(s_1\mathbf1_{L-2}-s_{2:L-1},s_{2:L-1}-s_L\mathbf1_{L-2})$. The auxiliary lower bound already imposes the feed-TSS engineering limit, so $g_{\rm eng,red}$ omits that duplicate row from $g_{\rm eng}$. The resulting nonlinear program (NLP) is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\vartheta,y,\widetilde X_F}\quad&J[\vartheta,\chi(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F);\varpi]\\
\text{subject to}\quad& f_{\rm sm}(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F)=0,\\
&\widetilde X_F=t_X^\top c_N,\quad \widetilde X_F\ge X_F^{\min},\\
&\vartheta\in\mathcal V,\quad y\ge0,\\
&g_{\rm env}(y)\le0,\\
&g_{\rm eng,red}[\vartheta,\chi(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F)]\le0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mechanisticnlp}
\end{equation}
Its selected feasible incumbent, when one exists, is denoted $(\vartheta_M,y_M,\widetilde X_{F,M})$. The auxiliary does not change a feasible solution but keeps reconstruction defined during the search.

Derivative-based NLP methods use derivatives of the objective and constraint functions \citep{BuzziFerraris2013}. Here, smooth approximations make the kinetic and settling equations differentiable for optimization. They are used only for minimum, maximum, positive-part, zero-denominator, and receiver-switch operations. A nonzero smoothing width changes the equations near a switch. The smooth endpoint must therefore be checked on the original nonsmooth model before its performance is compared with the surrogate-selected decision. Both routes use that same reference model for replay.

The reference model also has a defined physical scope. Its scalar-layer clarifier tracks aggregate solids, and particulate outlet components are reconstructed with the steady feed composition. Time integration uses a backward differentiation formula (BDF) for stiff equations \citep{CurtissHirschfelder1952}. In this study, integration provides a numerical route to the nonsmooth steady equations. The model does not track the transient inventory of every particulate species in every layer. A reference replay is an independently converged solution of those nonsmoothed equations. Stability refers only to this reduced numerical state model. The methodology below specifies how data from this model are used to fit the surrogate and how both routes are evaluated on the common reference.

\section{Methodology}

The evaluation follows seven ordered steps. First, the mechanistic model generates accepted plant states over the operating and influent domains. Second, a fixed analysis sample is divided into model-development and post-selection holdout sets. Third, grouped cross-validation selects the raw-response and overflow-solids regressions. Fourth, out-of-fold predictions are projected and used to set trust limits. Fifth, the surrogate and direct mechanistic routes select operating decisions. Sixth, both decisions are replayed on the same nonsmooth mechanistic model. Seventh, eligible pairs are compared by exact-reference objective, controls, and complete calculation time. Prediction assessment, decision replay, and paired comparison therefore remain separate.

\subsection{Case-study system and operating domain}

The case study uses Activated Sludge Model No. 2d with two-step nitrification (ASM2d-TSN). It has $F=20$ components, $F_S=F_X=10$, and $K=5$ named invariants. Five equal-volume reactors are followed by a ten-layer clarifier. Reactors 1 and 2 are unaerated. Reactors 3--5 have independent aeration controls. The seven free operating coordinates are
\begin{equation}
\vartheta=(H,a_3,a_4,a_5,r_I,r_R,w)^\top\in\R^7,
\label{eq:casecontrols}
\end{equation}
where $h_i=H/5$, $a_1=a_2=0$, $k_La_i=47a_i$ d$^{-1}$ for $i=3,4,5$, and $\xi_i=0$ for every stage.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Case-study topology, geometry, and free-coordinate bounds.}
\label{tab:domain}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Quantity & Value or range & Case-study role\\
\midrule
$Q_0$ & 10,000 m$^3$ d$^{-1}$ & Fresh-flow basis\\
$N$; $L$; $\ell_F$ & 5; 10; 5 & Reactors; layers; feed layer\\
$f_i^V$ & $1/5$ & Equal reactor-volume fractions\\
$A_{\rm cl}$; depth & 1500 m$^2$; 4.0 m & Clarifier geometry\\
$H$ & 6--36 h & Total nominal HRT\\
$a_3,a_4,a_5$ & independently 0--1 & Stage aeration settings\\
$r_I$ & 0--4 & MLR ratio\\
$r_R$ & 0.25--1.25 & RAS ratio\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Five equal-volume stages provide a compact anoxic--aerobic train for the comparative calculation. Ten 0.4-m clarifier layers resolve the specified 4-m depth without making the layer count a structural constant. The operating box spans low-to-high residence time, aeration, recycle, and wasting settings while preserving $q_E>0$ and $q_U>0$. These choices define the case-study domain and are not universal design bounds.

For this case, extended ICSOR uses 27 inputs, 406 features, and 161 response coordinates. The mechanistic route has 110 state coordinates. The projection imposes 76 equalities and 12 physical inequalities. Response non-negativity increases the total inequality count to 173. These dimensions follow from the declared plant configuration and are not fixed properties of the method.

The objective gives the largest individual weight to effluent quality while retaining explicit penalties for resource use. Its priorities are
\begin{equation}
\varpi=(0.50,0.15,0.20,0.05,0.05,0.05)^\top,
\qquad \varpi_Q=\tfrac14\mathbf1_4.
\label{eq:caseweights}
\end{equation}
Aeration receives the largest resource coefficient because oxygen transfer is an important energy demand in the treatment train \citep{Leu2012}. HRT represents installed biological volume. The remaining resource coefficients apply to MLR, RAS, and wasted-solids production. The coefficients are trade-off parameters rather than percentages of the attained objective. The direct route uses $D_{T,M}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_j)$ for positive effluent standard deviations computed from the model-development set. The surrogate route uses $D_{T,S}=\operatorname{diag}[\max(1,\sigma_j)]$. Both exact-reference objectives use $D_{T,M}$. The fixed aeration normalization is $E_A^{\rm ref}=\sum_{i=3}^{5}V_i(H^U)(47\,\mathrm{d}^{-1})=423{,}000$ m$^3$ d$^{-1}$.

Both optimization routes impose $8\le\mathrm{SRT}\le30$ d, $\mathrm{SLR}\le100$ kg TSS m$^{-2}$ d$^{-1}$, $X_U\le15{,}000$ g TSS m$^{-3}$, $X_N\ge1$ g TSS m$^{-3}$, and $\dot M_X/Q_0\ge1$ g TSS m$^{-3}$. The final replay eligibility check retains every limit except the 8-d minimum SRT. A replay below 8 d is reported but is not excluded for that reason alone. The decisions must still be interpreted as solutions obtained with the 8-d search constraint. The wasted-solids objective uses $X_U^{\rm ref}=15{,}000$ g TSS m$^{-3}$. SOR is reported but is not constrained because the declared $Q_0$, $A_{\rm cl}$, and $w$ range gives $6.33\le\mathrm{SOR}\le6.66$ m d$^{-1}$. The complete influent domain, reporting map, model constants, initial states, and numerical generation settings are provided in the Supplementary Material, Sections~S1 and S2.

\subsection{Mechanistic generation and analysis sample}

The mechanistic design spans the joint seven-control and twenty-influent domain through Latin-hypercube sampling \citep{McKay1979}. Every candidate is solved from two declared initial states on the nonsmooth five-reactor and ten-layer model. A state is accepted only when both starts give finite and non-negative states and pass conservation, domain, layer-residual, stability, and branch-agreement checks. The full state is then mapped deterministically to the 161-coordinate response used for fitting.

Generation stopped after 18,211 completed attempts. It produced 16,714 accepted states and 1,497 rejected attempts. The analysis sample selects 10,000 accepted states without replacement after ordering the pool by original candidate index. The first 8,000 selected states form the model-development set. They are used for model selection, final fitting, out-of-fold projection assessment, and trust calibration. The remaining 2,000 states form the descriptive post-selection holdout. Rejected attempts remain in the generation ledger and do not enter fitting or assessment.

Combining initial survivors with replacement candidates means that the accepted pool is not one global Latin hypercube. It is also conditioned on numerical solvability and may underrepresent regions where the mechanistic checks fail. The holdout is a partition of the same accepted simulation stream as the model-development set. It is not independent confirmation. The final response definition, overflow closure, numerical procedures, and engineering eligibility rules were not specified independently of all inspected data. The holdout and scenario results therefore provide descriptive post-selection evidence. Holdout and optimization responses do not alter the fitted models, preprocessing, trust limits, objective priorities, engineering limits, or solver settings.

\subsection{Model fitting, projection assessment, and trust calibration}

All responses from one simulated plant remain in the same fold. The 8,000 model-development states are divided reproducibly into five folds of 1,600 states. Centers and scales are recomputed within each fitting fold. The raw multiresponse regression uses ridge regularization with an unpenalized intercept. Its score is the standardized root mean squared error (RMSE) over the complete raw response. The one-standard-error rule selects the largest penalty whose mean score is within one standard error of the minimum \citep{Hastie2009}. Projection metrics do not influence this selection.

The scalar logarithmic overflow-solids model uses the same folds, preprocessing, penalty grid, and selection rule. Its score is calculated on the logarithmic target. After selection, both models are refitted on all 8,000 model-development states. Out-of-fold predictions are used for projection assessment and trust calibration. Final fits are used only for the holdout, optimization, and selected-decision evaluations.

Every projected response is checked independently for equality feasibility, inequality feasibility, non-negativity, dual feasibility, stationarity, and complementarity. Scientific admission requires finite and audited out-of-fold projections for all model-development states. It also requires raw normalized RMSE below one for the complete response and the clarifier-inventory coordinate. A failed gate does not trigger refitting.

The trust region uses four diagnostics. They measure standardized projection correction, regularized feature leverage, particulate-recovery deviation, and smooth reactor-balance residual. Correction, recovery, and reactor limits are the out-of-fold 95th percentiles from the model-development set. The leverage limit is the maximum model-development leverage under the final feature map. All four limits are fixed before optimization. The post-selection holdout reports raw and projected RMSE, mean absolute error (MAE), bias, normalized RMSE (nRMSE), and coefficient of determination ($R^2$) for each process block and the complete response. Overflow TSS is also assessed on logarithmic and concentration scales. The detailed fitting equations, sampling seeds, projection certificates, error bounds, scales, and tolerances are provided in the Supplementary Material, Sections~S3 and S4.

\subsection{Operating searches and convergence assessment}

The nominal influent is the midpoint of each declared influent interval. Both routes use the seven controls in Equation~\eqref{eq:casecontrols}, the bounds in Table~\ref{tab:domain}, the objective priorities in Equation~\eqref{eq:caseweights}, and the engineering limits stated above. Each primary search starts at the center of the normalized operating box and explores one local basin.

The surrogate search evaluates only the seven controls. Each distinct trial receives a cold solution of the projection QP and a complete independent audit \citep{Stellato2020,Boyd2004}. Response derivatives are obtained from the active-set Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) system when rank, conditioning, complementarity, and derivative checks pass. Sequential least-squares programming (SLSQP) then performs the outer search. If the derivative chain cannot be validated, Constrained Optimization BY Quadratic Approximations (COBYQA) continues with function values from the same audited projection.

A surrogate candidate receives first-order stationarity evidence only when the derivative, projection, and upper-level KKT audits pass. Otherwise, complete feasible-direction polls at two declared resolutions test for sufficient decrease. These finite polls provide evidence only for the tested directions and resolutions. They do not establish stationarity or local optimality. Neither convergence tier establishes global optimality.

The direct route uses the Interior Point OPTimizer (IPOPT) with three smoothing stages and a full-state endpoint audit. If the primary direct solve fails, one recovery may start from the same case's certified surrogate control and repeat the unchanged continuation sequence. This recovery is conditional on route $S$ and is not an independent search. A validated feasible candidate remains reportable when a search budget is exhausted or stationarity is unresolved. A case without a validated candidate remains a failure. The full solver settings, poll construction, smoothing definitions, and recovery rules are provided in the Supplementary Material, Section~S5.

\subsection{Exact replay and paired decision comparison}

Native objectives cannot by themselves separate decision quality from response error or route-specific normalization. Each available selected candidate $k\in\{S,M\}$ is therefore replayed from two initial states on the nonsmooth ten-layer mechanistic model. The replay must pass state agreement, mass conservation, non-negativity, layer residual, domain, reduced-stability, and branch checks. Boundary-ambiguous branch disagreement may be qualified only when state agreement and all physical checks pass. The full replay state is mapped to $\chi_{{\rm ref},k}$ using Equations~\eqref{eq:state} and \eqref{eq:inventory}.

Native responses are $\chi_{{\rm nat},S}=\widehat\chi_S$ and $\chi_{{\rm nat},M}=\chi_{{\rm sm},M}$. Extended ICSOR is also evaluated, wherever defined, at both selected controls. Let $J_{{\rm nat},k}$ use the native normalization of route $k$ and let $J_{\rm ref}$ use $D_{T,M}$. The objective comparisons are
\begin{align}
\Delta J_{{\rm nat-ref},k}&=J_{{\rm nat},k}(\vartheta_k,\chi_{{\rm nat},k})-J_{\rm ref}(\vartheta_k,\chi_{{\rm ref},k}),\notag\\
\Delta J_{S-M}&=J_{\rm ref}(\vartheta_S,\chi_{{\rm ref},S})-J_{\rm ref}(\vartheta_M,\chi_{{\rm ref},M}).
\label{eq:comparisons}
\end{align}
Each valid, physically audited replay retains its objective and components even when an engineering safeguard is violated. Such a candidate is reported but excluded from paired comparison. A pair is eligible only when both candidates are feasible in their optimization models and both exact replays pass the physical, layer-envelope, and retained engineering checks. The eligibility check does not impose a minimum SRT. The objective difference is not termed regret because neither route has a global optimality certificate. Symmetric relative objective difference and normalized control root mean square (RMS) and maximum differences accompany each eligible pair. These casewise checks do not establish smooth and nonsmooth model equivalence over the full operating domain.

\subsection{Influent scenarios, timing, and failure accounting}

Ten influent vectors from a separate 20-dimensional Latin hypercube provide post-selection stress cases. They do not enter fitting, calibration, or tuning. Both routes are attempted for the nominal influent and for every stress case. Reference replay is performed whenever a selected decision is available. The recorded outputs include route status, controls, treatment responses, clarifier inventory and layer profiles, engineering measures, objective components, projection correction, and solve time. These cases show sensitivity within the evaluated set and do not establish population-level reliability.

Timing separates regression, projection, complete surrogate search and certification, primary direct optimization, conditional direct recovery, and exact replay. Aggregate timing comparisons use the ten stress cases and exclude the nominal case. Both routes use the same processor allocation and thread count. Workload and failure records accompany all timing summaries. A solver success flag is insufficient for acceptance. \section{Conclusions}

This study formulates a plant-wide extension of ICSOR and a direct smooth mechanistic comparator for the same activated sludge system. The objective is to assess whether physical reconciliation can support useful operating decisions at a lower repeated optimization cost. Extended ICSOR predicts the connected unit responses and reconciles them with network balances, conserved inventories, and non-negativity. Outlet component flows close the recycle, and the aggregate clarifier inventory supplies the storage term needed for whole-plant SRT. A separate log regression provides a positive overflow-TSS closure. The surrogate does not predict internal clarifier layers.

The projection makes the smallest standardized correction satisfying the declared physical constraints, empirical overflow equality, and outlet-derived inventory bounds. These requirements prevent specific inconsistencies but do not enforce all kinetic and settling equations. Numerical audits likewise distinguish a correctly solved projection from convergence of the operating search. Derivative-based first-order evidence and finite-resolution feasible polls are reported separately, and neither establishes global optimality.

The evaluation addresses prediction accuracy, the validity of selected operating decisions, and the trade-off between decision quality and computational cost as separate questions. Both routes share the operating controls, objective priorities, and exact nonsmooth reference. Native normalization and final engineering eligibility are kept distinct. Replay checks nonlinear kinetics, all ten clarifier layer balances, stability, and engineering feasibility. The 2,000-row holdout and ten influent scenarios provide descriptive post-selection evidence rather than independent confirmation. The paired comparison does not apply a minimum-SRT eligibility threshold, although the selected decisions were obtained from optimization problems that included the 8-d lower bound. The present formulation does not establish an accuracy or speed advantage before the required result tables are complete.
