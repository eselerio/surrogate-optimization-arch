\begin{abstract}
Operational optimization of activated sludge plants requires repeated evaluation of interacting biological, recycle, and settling processes. This study develops a plant-wide extension of invariant-constrained second-order regression and compares surrogate-based optimization with direct smooth mechanistic optimization. The objective is to assess whether physical reconciliation can support useful operating decisions while reducing repeated optimization cost. The surrogate predicts mixer and reactor concentrations, Clarifier outlet component flows, and aggregate Clarifier solids inventory. A constrained projection reconciles these predictions with recycle mixing, conserved inventories, Clarifier balances, outlet-derived inventory bounds, and non-negativity. A separate log-scale regression supplies a positive overflow solids concentration. The case study uses five equal-volume reactors, independent aeration in reactors 3--5, a ten-layer mechanistic Clarifier, and 16,714 accepted mechanistic states. Model fitting uses plant-grouped five-fold cross-validation. Prediction assessment and ten influent scenarios provide descriptive post-selection evidence. Numerical audits distinguish first-order stationarity from finite-resolution convergence evidence. Every available selected decision is evaluated on the same nonsmooth mechanistic reference to assess engineering feasibility and objective performance. The comparison separates prediction accuracy, physical consistency, decision quality, and computational cost. It does not assume that enforcing selected physical constraints reproduces the full mechanistic model or establishes global optimality.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item A constrained second-order surrogate represents the complete recycling plant.
\item A log-scale regression supplies a positive overflow solids concentration.
\item Surrogate and mechanistic optimization use identical controls and limits.
\item Projection sensitivities support auditable surrogate optimization.
\item Both routes are judged on one exact ten-layer reference model.

\section{Introduction}

An activated sludge plant forms one recycling system through mixed-liquor recycle (MLR), return activated sludge (RAS), waste activated sludge (WAS), biological conversion, aeration, and secondary clarification. Changes in recycle, aeration, or wasting affect reactor conditions, power demand, solids age, treated-water quality, and the solids returned to the biology. These coupled effects prevent the operating decisions from being treated independently. Mathematical programming provides a systematic way to select these interacting operating decisions together while balancing treatment performance, resource use, and operating constraints. Activated Sludge Models describe the biological and chemical processes through coupled nonlinear dynamic mass-balance equations. Settling models describe clarification and thickening \citep{Henze2006,Takacs1991}. Their predicted steady state is commonly obtained by integrating these equations forward in time until the transient changes become negligible. During operational optimization, this steady-state calculation must be repeated for the many candidate operating conditions considered by the mathematical program. These repeated calculations can require substantial computation.

Statistical surrogate modeling offers an alternative to repeatedly solving the mechanistic equations during optimization. A surrogate may be fitted to input--output data obtained from experiments, generated by a mechanistic model, or assembled from both sources. It is then used to predict the process responses for new operating conditions. Surrogate models are widely used in process systems engineering when direct simulation is too computationally demanding for repeated evaluation during feasibility analysis and optimization \citep{Bhosekar2018}.

Statistical surrogates can be evaluated much faster than mechanistic models. This computational advantage does not ensure physically meaningful predictions. An unconstrained regression may predict a negative concentration or fail to preserve a conserved inventory between reactors. This concern is particularly important in wastewater treatment because the removal processes bring the treated-water concentrations of many components close to zero. Zero is the lower boundary of their physical range. Near this boundary, a small absolute prediction error relative to the influent or mixed liquor can be a large relative error in the treated water. For example, an error of 1 g m$^{-3}$ is only 1\% of an influent concentration of 100 g m$^{-3}$. The same error is 100\% of a treated-water concentration of 1 g m$^{-3}$. A regression fitted directly to concentration data may therefore appear accurate over the overall concentration range while producing poor or physically implausible treated-water predictions. This risk increases during surrogate-based operational optimization of activated sludge systems. The optimizer deliberately searches for operating conditions with the most favorable surrogate predictions. This search may exploit prediction errors \citep{Bhosekar2018}.

These risks motivate the explicit enforcement of physical constraints on statistical surrogate predictions. Such constraints do more than improve average prediction accuracy. They restrict the predicted response to states that satisfy known process requirements. Activated sludge and wastewater studies have begun to address parts of this problem through hybrid models and constrained uses of surrogates. \citet{Li2024} embedded governing-equation residuals in physics-informed neural networks for unit-operation models, including an activated sludge reactor, and reported lower data requirements and better generalization across loading conditions than conventional machine learning models. At the operational level, \citet{Bernardelli2020} placed a neuro-fuzzy process model inside real-time model predictive control to select aeration while jointly pursuing nutrient-removal and energy objectives. At the broader wastewater-process level, \citet{Durkin2024} used data-driven unit surrogates within constrained superstructure optimization for resource recovery. These formulations retain process knowledge or impose operating requirements during decision making, but soft equation residuals and optimization-level constraints do not guarantee that every unconstrained statistical prediction satisfies exact balances and bounds. To the author's knowledge, \citet{Selerio2026} reported the first explicit output-space projection of a statistical activated sludge surrogate onto mass-conservation and component-nonnegativity constraints. That work projected second-order regression predictions onto a feasible set defined by these requirements. It showed that a statistical model can retain its simple input--output form while ensuring that every projected prediction satisfies the enforced physical constraints.

Related studies outside activated sludge modeling show that physical constraints can be introduced at different stages of surrogate construction. Analytic conservation constraints have been incorporated into neural network output mappings for atmospheric convection \citep{Beucler2021}. For atmospheric photochemistry, \citet{SturmWexler2020} formulated conserved fluxes as learning targets. \citet{SturmWexler2022} later embedded the stoichiometric mapping as a fixed layer that conserves atoms to numerical precision. Other studies have used constrained training data and a mass-balance penalty to reduce conservation errors \citep{Ruckstuhl2021}. Physical consistency can also be imposed after an unconstrained model has made a prediction. \citet{SturmSilva2025} applied a species-weighted correction that enforces atom conservation. \citet{Valente2025} projected surrogate outputs onto manifolds defined by conservation laws. These applications concern atmospheric and plasma systems. The constraint mechanisms they develop are also relevant to statistical surrogates of wastewater treatment processes.

Important gaps therefore remain. The Invariant-Constrained Second-Order Regression (ICSOR) formulation of \citet{Selerio2026} applies to the response of a single continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR). It does not represent the mixer, reactor train, secondary Clarifier, and recycle streams as one coupled response. This limitation prevents direct plant-wide application. Physical consistency within one CSTR does not ensure recycle mixing, component conservation across clarification, or consistency between the Clarifier overflow and underflow. No study was identified that combines hard output-space physical reconciliation of a statistical activated sludge surrogate with plant-wide operational optimization. Such a demonstration is necessary because an optimizer can exploit small prediction errors when selecting operating controls. A credible plant-wide approach must preserve the physical connections among units, enforce engineering limits during the search, and verify the selected decisions against a mechanistic reference. It must also distinguish physical feasibility of a surrogate prediction from plant-wide prediction accuracy and decision quality.

This study develops and evaluates a plant-wide extension of ICSOR to address the computational cost and physical reliability of surrogate-based operational optimization. The objective is to determine whether a physically constrained statistical surrogate can support the selection of operating conditions that balance treatment performance and resource use. The extended model represents the mixer, reactor train, Clarifier, and recycle streams jointly and reconciles their predictions with plant-wide physical constraints. Three research questions guide the evaluation. First, how does this reconciliation affect physical consistency and prediction accuracy across the plant? Second, do the surrogate-selected operating conditions satisfy the engineering limits and retain their predicted performance when evaluated with the mechanistic model? Third, how do decision quality and computational cost compare with direct mechanistic optimization under the same operating choices, objective, and engineering limits? Separate prediction assessment and mechanistic evaluation of the selected decisions address these questions. The assessment remains descriptive because previously inspected data informed model revisions. Satisfaction of the imposed physical constraints is therefore assessed separately from prediction accuracy and decision quality.

\section{Theory and calculation}

The modeling problem is to select reactor capacity, aeration, recycle, and wasting settings for a coupled activated sludge plant at steady state. Each choice affects both local unit conditions and the material returned through the recycle streams. The formulation therefore begins with hydraulic and component balances that connect the mixer, reactors, and Clarifier. These relations define the physical requirements used to reconcile the statistical predictions. The resulting plant response supplies the treatment and resource measures used in a common optimization objective and set of engineering limits. The section then develops the surrogate optimization problem, its numerical verification, and a direct mechanistic comparator. This common basis allows the two routes to be assessed for operating performance and computational cost without assuming that physical reconciliation reproduces the full mechanistic model.

\subsection{Plant configuration and hydraulic balances}

The hydraulic balances establish how each recycle setting changes the flow through the treatment units. Consider $N\ge1$ completely mixed biological stages followed by an $L\ge3$ layer Clarifier. Soluble and particulate components are distinguished because they behave differently during settling. Component vectors contain $F=F_S+F_X$ entries. The matrices $E_S\in\R^{F_S\times F}$ and $E_X\in\R^{F_X\times F}$ select the disjoint soluble and particulate entries, so $E_S^\top E_S+E_X^\top E_X=I_F$ and $E_SE_X^\top=0$. The non-negative total suspended solids (TSS) map $t_X$ has zero soluble entries. Figure~\ref{fig:flowsheet} shows the plant connections. The final reactor supplies both the MLR stream and the Clarifier. Clarifier underflow is split into RAS, which returns internally, and WAS, which leaves the plant.

Normalizing every flow by the fresh-influent flow $Q_0$ gives
\begin{equation}
q_P=1+r_I+r_R,\qquad q_C=1+r_R,\qquad
q_U=r_R+w,\qquad q_E=1-w.
\label{eq:flows}
\end{equation}
The Clarifier identity $q_C=q_E+q_U$ follows immediately. The operating domain requires $r_I\ge0$, $r_R\ge0$, $0\le w<1$, and $q_U=r_R+w>0$, so both outlet concentrations can later be recovered safely from their mass flows. At the mixer, fresh feed, MLR, and RAS meet. Dividing its component balance by $Q_0$ gives
\begin{equation}
q_Pm=x+r_Ic_N+r_Rc_U.
\label{eq:mixer}
\end{equation}

Recycle changes the flow through a reactor without changing its volume. The nominal hydraulic retention time (HRT) based on fresh flow must therefore be distinguished from the actual stage residence time. Define
\begin{equation}
h_i=\frac{24V_i}{Q_0},\qquad H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_i,\quad
d_i=\frac{Q_0q_P}{V_i}=\frac{24q_P}{h_i},
\label{eq:stagehydraulics}
\end{equation}
where $h_i$ and $H$ are in hours and $d_i$ is in d$^{-1}$. The actual stage residence time is $h_i/q_P$ hours. Increasing recycle at fixed volume therefore increases dilution and reduces the time spent in each pass through a reactor. Individual stage volumes may be varied through $h_i$, or fixed volume fractions may be imposed through $h_i=f_i^V H$ with $\sum_i f_i^V=1$. Equal tanks correspond to $f_i^V=1/N$.

The optimization variables must correspond to the choices available in the study. Let $\vartheta\in\R^{d_\vartheta}$ contain only these freely varied quantities. They may include selected $h_i$, aeration settings $a_i$, stage-addition rates, and the three recycle and wasting ratios. A stage addition represented only by a source term is assumed to have negligible carrier flow. If its liquid flow is not negligible relative to $Q_0$, that stream must also enter the hydraulic balances and change the downstream flow and dilution rates. Fixed settings remain parameters rather than zero-variance regression inputs. This definition avoids fitting coefficients for settings that never vary.

\subsection{Reactor balances and conserved inventories}

Reactor balances connect the operating settings to biological conversion and provide conservation relations for the surrogate. In reactor $i$, material enters from the preceding stage, leaves at the train flow, and is transformed by biological and chemical processes. Oxygen transfer and declared external additions provide separate sources. With $c_0=m$, the steady component balance is
\begin{equation}
0=d_i(c_{i-1}-c_i)+\nu^\top\rho_i(c_i;\vartheta)
+\omega_i(a_i,c_i)e_{S_O}+\xi_i(\vartheta),
\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.
\label{eq:cstr}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu\in\R^{n_p\times F}$ stores the stoichiometric changes caused by $n_p$ processes, and $\rho_i\in\R_+^{n_p}$ contains their volumetric rates. The vector $e_{S_O}$ selects dissolved oxygen (DO), and $\omega_i=k_La_i(a_i)(S_O^*-c_{i,S_O})$ describes oxygen transfer. The term $\xi_i$ represents an explicitly modeled stage addition with negligible carrier flow. Every term has units of component concentration per day. Retaining $\xi_i$ ensures that an optimized carbon or chemical dose enters the material balance. The combined reaction and source terms must not drive a component below zero when its concentration reaches zero. This condition preserves non-negative concentrations in the dynamic model.

The surrogate should preserve conserved inventories without solving every reaction-rate equation during prediction. Some linear combinations of components are changed neither by the reaction network nor by oxygen transfer. Let $A\in\R^{K\times F}$ contain independent, physically identified inventory rows satisfying
\begin{equation}
A\nu^\top=0,\qquad Ae_{S_O}=0,\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(A)=K,\qquad \|A_{k,:}\|_2=1.
\label{eq:invariants}
\end{equation}
Premultiplication of Equation~\eqref{eq:cstr} by $A$ removes the reaction and aeration terms and gives the stagewise inventory balance
\begin{equation}
A(c_i-c_{i-1})=d_i^{-1}A\xi_i(\vartheta),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.
\label{eq:rails}
\end{equation}
When $\xi_i=0$, the quantity $Ac_i$ is constant across the stage. Reactions can redistribute a conserved inventory among components but cannot create or remove it. These relations prevent the surrogate from improving a predicted treatment response by violating an inventory balance. If a stage dose adds a conserved quantity, Equation~\eqref{eq:rails} accounts for that addition. Its right-hand side can remain zero only when $A\xi_i=0$.

\subsection{Clarifier outlet balances and aggregate inventory}

At the Clarifier, component flow is more numerically useful than underflow concentration because $q_U$ may be small. Define
\begin{equation}
g_E=q_Ec_E,\qquad g_U=q_Uc_U.
\label{eq:massflows}
\end{equation}
The component balance around the complete vessel is then
\begin{equation}
q_Cc_N=g_E+g_U.
\label{eq:clarifierbalance}
\end{equation}
Because $q_E,q_U>0$, the concentrations are recovered as $c_E=g_E/q_E$ and $c_U=g_U/q_U$. Using flows in the balance avoids dividing by a small underflow ratio within the conservation constraint.

Soluble material follows the liquid, whereas particulate material may be concentrated into the underflow. The linear relations retained in the projection are
\begin{equation}
E_Sg_U=q_UE_Sc_N,\qquad E_Xg_U\ge q_UE_Xc_N.
\label{eq:clarificationrules}
\end{equation}
The first relation, together with Equation~\eqref{eq:clarifierbalance}, also gives $E_Sg_E=q_EE_Sc_N$. Thus the Clarifier does not remove soluble material through settling. The second relation prevents an underflow particulate concentration below the feed concentration. With non-negative outlet flows, it bounds each particulate recovery between $q_U/q_C$ and one. Applying the non-negative TSS map and the total Clarifier balance then gives $X_E\le X_N\le X_U$.

Outlet concentrations determine the returned and discharged solids, but they do not determine the solids stored inside the Clarifier. This stored mass is needed to calculate whole-plant solids age. Let $s_\ell$ be the mechanistic TSS concentration in layer $\ell$, numbered from the water surface ($\ell=1$) to the hopper ($\ell=L$). The endpoint concentrations follow from the outlet component flows as
\begin{equation}
X_E=s_1=\frac{t_X^\top g_E}{q_E},\qquad
X_U=s_L=\frac{t_X^\top g_U}{q_U}.
\label{eq:layerendpoints}
\end{equation}
The mechanistic acceptance envelope
\begin{equation}
X_E\le s_\ell\le X_U,\qquad \ell=2,\ldots,L-1,
\label{eq:layerenvelope}
\end{equation}
keeps every internal layer between the two outlet concentrations. It is intentionally an endpoint envelope, not a claim of layer-by-layer monotonicity. The nonlinear settling-flux balances, which determine the detailed shape, remain part of the mechanistic model. With positive layer volumes, the layer values determine the physical inventory
\begin{equation}
M_{\rm cl}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}V_{{\rm cl},\ell}s_\ell,
\qquad V_{\rm cl}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}V_{{\rm cl},\ell}.
\label{eq:inventory}
\end{equation}
Only this scalar inventory is retained in the statistical response. Eliminating the unneeded internal layer coordinates from Equations~\eqref{eq:layerendpoints}--\eqref{eq:inventory} gives the tight interval
\begin{align}
\underline M_{\rm cl}&=(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},L})X_E+V_{{\rm cl},L}X_U,\notag\\
\overline M_{\rm cl}&=V_{{\rm cl},1}X_E+(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},1})X_U,\notag\\
\underline M_{\rm cl}&\le M_{\rm cl}\le\overline M_{\rm cl}.
\label{eq:inventoryenvelope}
\end{align}
The interval is necessary and sufficient for the existence of at least one profile satisfying the endpoint envelope. Every intermediate inventory can be obtained by assigning a common admissible value to the internal layers. These bounds prevent the predicted stored mass from contradicting the predicted outlet concentrations. They retain useful storage information without requiring a statistical prediction for every layer. An admissible inventory does not establish that any corresponding profile satisfies the nonlinear settling-flux equations. That check remains with the mechanistic model.

\subsection{Extended ICSOR raw operational-response surrogate}

Plant-wide optimization requires a response that connects the recycle streams and supplies every objective and constraint calculation. The extended ICSOR surrogate, designated route $S$, therefore predicts the mixer and reactor states, outlet component flows, and aggregate inventory together. Its response vector is ordered in process sequence as
\begin{equation}
\chi=\col(m,c_1,\ldots,c_N,g_E,g_U,M_{\rm cl})
\in\R^{n_\chi},\qquad n_\chi=(N+3)F+1.
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
The endpoint TSS values remain recoverable from $g_E$ and $g_U$, while $M_{\rm cl}$ preserves the storage information required for solids retention time (SRT). The full $L$-layer profile remains in the mechanistic target from which this reduced response is constructed.

The regression must represent interactions among influent composition and operating settings. For example, the effect of aeration can depend on the load entering the reactors. Centering and scaling the inputs prevents their different units and magnitudes from dominating the fit. Let $z=\col[D_{\vartheta,{\rm fit}}^{-1}(\vartheta-\bar\vartheta),D_{x,{\rm fit}}^{-1}(x-\bar x)]\in\R^{d_z}$, where $d_z=d_\vartheta+F$ and the means and positive diagonal scales are computed from the development data. To include curvature and pairwise interactions without duplicate terms, form
\begin{equation}
r(z)=\col[z,\vech(zz^\top)],\qquad
\phi_S(\vartheta,x)=\col[1,D_{r,{\rm fit}}^{-1}\{r(z)-\bar r\}]
\in\R^{n_{\phi,S}},
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
where $\vech$ lists one triangular copy of the symmetric quadratic matrix. Hence
\begin{equation}
n_{\phi,S}=1+d_z+\frac{d_z(d_z+1)}2.
\label{eq:featurecount}
\end{equation}
The dimension now adapts automatically if another stage control is freed.

With development response mean $\bar\chi$, positive scale $D_\chi$, and fitted coefficient matrix $\widehat C_{S,\gamma_S}$, the route-$S$ raw response is
\begin{equation}
\chi_{{\rm raw},S}(\vartheta,x)=\bar\chi+D_\chi\widehat C_{S,\gamma_S}\phi_S(\vartheta,x).
\label{eq:raw}
\end{equation}
The ridge parameter $\gamma_S$ penalizes large coefficients to stabilize the fit when features are correlated. Equation~\eqref{eq:raw} is still an unconstrained regression prediction. It does not yet satisfy a sign or balance requirement.

Overflow TSS spans a strongly asymmetric range. Small absolute errors can be important when the treated-water concentration is low, yet contribute little to a fit over the full plant response. A separate logarithmic model gives this endpoint a relative-error scale without changing the raw multiresponse fit. Define the dimensionless development target
\begin{equation}
\ell_E=\log\!\left(\frac{X_E}{X_\star}\right),
\qquad X_\star=1\ {\rm mg\,L^{-1}},
\label{eq:logtarget}
\end{equation}
and fit a separate scalar ridge coefficient vector $\widehat\beta_{E,\gamma_E}$ on the same complete second-order input family. Its positive concentration-scale prediction is
\begin{equation}
\widehat X_{E,\log}(\vartheta,x)
=X_\star\exp\!\left\{\widehat\beta_{E,\gamma_E}^{\top}
\phi_S(\vartheta,x)\right\}>0.
\label{eq:logclosure}
\end{equation}
The logarithm assigns the same discrepancy to a twofold error at low or high concentration. Exponentiation also prevents a negative fitted endpoint. Under squared log error, direct exponentiation estimates a conditional geometric center rather than an unbiased concentration-scale conditional mean. No post hoc smearing factor is introduced. The positive prediction varies with all declared controls and influent coordinates and is not a mechanistic lower bound. No clipping rule or fixed overflow-to-feed settling ratio is imposed. The closure regression is fitted and selected only from development rows. Grouped out-of-fold values are used wherever a development prediction enters projection assessment or trust calibration.

\subsection{System-wide invariant-constraint projection}

A joint projection is needed because correcting one unit independently can make its outlet inconsistent with the next unit or recycle stream. The projection adjusts the assembled response to satisfy the network relations simultaneously. It keeps the derived physical balances distinct from the empirical overflow closure. Define $\delta_i(\vartheta)=d_i^{-1}A\xi_i(\vartheta)$ and let $\mathcal H(\vartheta)\chi=\mathfrak b(\vartheta,x)$ represent
\begin{equation}
\col\!\left(
\begin{array}{c}
q_Pm-r_Ic_N-(r_R/q_U)g_U-x\\
\{A(c_i-c_{i-1})-\delta_i\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad c_0=m\\
g_E+g_U-q_Cc_N\\
E_Sg_U-q_UE_Sc_N
\end{array}\right)=0.
\label{eq:Hbase}
\end{equation}
The network balances alone do not determine the degree of solids separation. The fitted overflow endpoint supplies this additional empirical relation through
\begin{equation}
t_X^\top g_E=q_E\widehat X_{E,\log}(\vartheta,x).
\label{eq:overflowclosure}
\end{equation}
At fixed $(\vartheta,x)$, the right-hand side is a known positive scalar, so Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure} is linear in $\chi$ and preserves the quadratic program (QP) structure. The complete equality operator $\mathcal H_{\log}(\vartheta)\chi=\mathfrak b_{\log}(\vartheta,x)$ collects Equations~\eqref{eq:Hbase} and \eqref{eq:overflowclosure}. There are
\begin{equation}
n_h=2F+NK+F_S+1
\label{eq:eqcount}
\end{equation}
rows covering the mixer balance, $N$ invariant transitions, the full Clarifier balance, soluble pass-through, and the scalar overflow-TSS closure. The closure fixes only total overflow TSS. It does not prescribe the particulate composition of $g_E$. That allocation is reconciled jointly with the raw component predictions and the remaining constraints.

Particulate densification and inventory bounds restrict responses that could satisfy the equalities but still contradict the adopted settling envelope. These inequalities are written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal G(\vartheta)\chi=
\col\!\left(
\begin{array}{c}
q_UE_Xc_N-E_Xg_U\\
q_U(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},L})t_X^\top g_E
+q_EV_{{\rm cl},L}t_X^\top g_U-q_Eq_UM_{\rm cl}\\
q_Eq_UM_{\rm cl}-q_UV_{{\rm cl},1}t_X^\top g_E
-q_E(V_{\rm cl}-V_{{\rm cl},1})t_X^\top g_U
\end{array}\right)\le0.
\label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
Thus $n_g=F_X+2$ inequality rows describe particulate densification and aggregate-inventory feasibility. The last two rows are the division-free form of Equation~\eqref{eq:inventoryenvelope} and remain linear in $\chi$ at fixed $\vartheta$. Non-negativity contributes another $n_\chi$ inequalities.

The correction is measured in standardized plant-response coordinates $D_\chi$, so that the large-magnitude inventory coordinate does not overwhelm a small nutrient coordinate. The projected displacement and response are
\begin{align}
\widehat u_S(\vartheta,x)=\argminop_{u\in\R^{n_\chi}}\quad&\tfrac12\|u\|_2^2
\label{eq:projection}\\
\text{subject to}\quad&
 D_b^{-1}\{\mathcal H_{\log}(\vartheta)[\chi_{{\rm raw},S}+D_\chi u]-\mathfrak b_{\log}(\vartheta,x)\}=0,\notag\\
&\chi_{{\rm raw},S}+D_\chi u\ge0,\notag\\
&D_g^{-1}\mathcal G(\vartheta)[\chi_{{\rm raw},S}+D_\chi u]\le0,\notag\\[1mm]
\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x)={}&\chi_{{\rm raw},S}(\vartheta,x)+D_\chi\widehat u_S(\vartheta,x).
\label{eq:projected}
\end{align}
$D_b$ and $D_g$ improve numerical conditioning without changing the feasible set. The overflow-closure row receives a positive scale fixed from development data, as do the other equality rows. The projection is applied once to the complete raw plant response. For example, correcting an underflow prediction must also preserve the associated return flow entering the mixer. Separate unit projections would not enforce this joint consistency. Whenever the constraint set is nonempty, the strictly convex and coercive objective gives a unique projected response. Because Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure} is fitted rather than a physical identity, feasibility is not guaranteed throughout the operating box. It is checked for every grouped out-of-fold development row, optimization evaluation, and reported holdout or selected-decision evaluation. An infeasible closure is recorded as a failed projection and is not clipped or relaxed.

Enforcing constraints does not by itself establish greater prediction accuracy. A projection error bound clarifies when an improvement is guaranteed. If a mechanistic reference $\chi_{\rm ref}$ satisfies the projection constraints exactly, Euclidean-projection geometry in standardized coordinates gives
\begin{equation}
\|D_\chi^{-1}(\widehat\chi_S-\chi_{\rm ref})\|_2^2
\le \|D_\chi^{-1}(\chi_{{\rm raw},S}-\chi_{\rm ref})\|_2^2
-\|\widehat u_S\|_2^2.
\label{eq:nonexpansive}
\end{equation}
Thus the correction cannot increase standardized squared error to a reduced mechanistic reference point that is actually in the projection set. A mechanistic target generally does not equal the fitted value in Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure}, so this guarantee is not presumed for the revised projection. The finite-distance bound introduced in the methodology provides the general audit. Small infeasibility is not treated as exact feasibility. Neither bound reconstructs a Clarifier profile or establishes satisfaction of all kinetic and nonlinear settling equations. Those equations are checked separately by full mechanistic replay.

\subsection{Engineering objective and operating constraints}

Lower effluent concentrations can require more reactor volume, aeration, recycle, or solids removal. The objective combines treatment and resource measures to make these competing requirements explicit. Their relative priorities are case-study choices rather than universal constants. Let $z_E=I_{\rm comp}c_E\in\R_+^4$ contain effluent chemical oxygen demand (COD), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and TSS. With positive normalization $D_T$ and quality priorities $\varpi_Q\ge0$, $\mathbf1^\top\varpi_Q=1$, define
\begin{equation}
j_Q=\varpi_Q^\top D_T^{-1}z_E.
\label{eq:qualitycomponent}
\end{equation}
Representative dimensionless resource terms are
\begin{align}
j_H&=\frac{H-H^L}{H^U-H^L},&
j_A&=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}V_i k_La_i}
{E_A^{\rm ref}},\notag\\
j_I&=\frac{r_I-r_I^L}{r_I^U-r_I^L},&
j_R&=\frac{r_R-r_R^L}{r_R^U-r_R^L},&
j_W&=\frac{w\,t_X^\top c_U}{w^UX_U^{\rm ref}}.
\label{eq:objectivecomponents}
\end{align}
$\mathcal A$ is the set of stages whose aeration can be nonzero. The fixed normalization $E_A^{\rm ref}>0$ is chosen before optimization. In the case study, it is the maximum value of $\sum_{i\in\mathcal A}V_i k_La_i$ over the declared operating box. The aeration term is therefore proportional to total installed oxygen-transfer capacity. Its denominator does not change when stage volumes are varied. This prevents an increase in capacity from being offset merely by rescaling the objective. These resource terms are engineering proxies rather than a monetary cost model. Additional declared operating terms can be included in the same manner. With $j=\col(j_Q,j_H,j_A,j_I,j_R,j_W)$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
J(\vartheta,\chi;\varpi)=\varpi^\top j(\vartheta,\chi),
\qquad \varpi\ge0,\quad \mathbf1^\top\varpi=1.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The vectors $\varpi$ and $\varpi_Q$ distinguish overall operating priorities from priorities among effluent-quality measures. Their values are stated and justified in the case study.

An objective alone could favor settings that exceed the plant's operating limits. Engineering constraints therefore restrict solids storage, Clarifier loading, and outlet conditions using the same response as the objective. Define $X_i=t_X^\top c_i$ and use $X_E$ and $X_U$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:layerendpoints}. The external solids loss rate is
\begin{equation}
\dot M_X=Q_0(q_EX_E+wX_U).
\end{equation}
The whole-plant SRT, surface overflow rate (SOR), and Clarifier solids loading rate (SLR) are then
\begin{align}
\mathrm{SRT}&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_iX_i+M_{\rm cl}}{\dot M_X},
\qquad \dot M_X\ge\epsilon_X>0,\label{eq:srt}\\
\mathrm{SOR}&=\frac{Q_0q_E}{A_{\rm cl}},\label{eq:sor}\\
\mathrm{SLR}&=\frac{10^{-3}Q_0q_CX_N}{A_{\rm cl}}.
\label{eq:slr}
\end{align}
The factor $10^{-3}$ converts grams to kilograms in SLR. Including $M_{\rm cl}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:srt} accounts for solids stored outside the biological reactors. Omitting this inventory would underestimate whole-plant SRT for the same solids loss rate. Upper limits on SRT, SOR, SLR, $X_U$, and any effluent targets, together with the positive external-solids-loss guard, form $g_{\rm eng}(\vartheta,\chi)\le0$. Their values are assigned in the case study. The SRT upper limit is imposed in cross-multiplied form after the positive-loss guard to avoid division by a small quantity. No minimum SRT is imposed. Finite SRT remains a descriptive operating metric, and the upper bound restricts excessive solids age.

Network feasibility does not prevent an optimizer from selecting a response that needs an unusually large correction or is inconsistent with reaction kinetics. The additional constraints $g_{{\rm trust},S}$ restrict such behavior using four diagnostics calibrated on development data. The standardized correction $\kappa_{{\rm corr},S}=\|\widehat u_S\|_2/\sqrt{n_\chi}$ measures how far projection moves the raw response. Regularized feature leverage measures support from the fitted input features. Deviation from a common particulate recovery tests consistency with the mechanistic outlet-composition assumption. The scaled smooth reactor-balance residual measures disagreement with reactor kinetics. For example, a response that conserves inventories can still require an implausible reaction rate at the selected aeration setting. These diagnostics provide additional screening rather than a guarantee of mechanistic accuracy. Their formulas and thresholds are given in the Supplementary Material. Thresholds use grouped development calculations without holdout or optimization responses. Nonlinear Clarifier behavior is checked only through the exact $L$-layer replay because the aggregate response cannot supply layer-flux residuals.

The extended-ICSOR operational problem combines these requirements as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\vartheta}\quad&J[\vartheta,\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x);\varpi]\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\vartheta\in\mathcal V,\\
&g_{\rm eng}[\vartheta,\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x)]\le0,\\
&g_{{\rm trust},S}[\vartheta,\chi_{{\rm raw},S},\widehat\chi_S]\le0,\\
&\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta,x)\text{ solves Equation~\eqref{eq:projection}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:bilevel}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal V$ contains the declared bounds and any stage-allocation equalities. The projected response is unique whenever the lower problem is feasible, but the upper problem remains generally nonconvex and piecewise smooth. Accordingly, $\vartheta_S$ denotes the best feasible candidate retained by the search, together with its reported convergence tier. It is not a certified global minimizer.

\subsection{Exact lower-level certificate and active-set sensitivity}

Every objective evaluation depends on a correctly solved projection. A numerical optimality certificate separates projection error from error in the regression or operating search. For fixed $(\vartheta,x)$, write the projection as
\begin{equation}
\min_u\tfrac12u^\top u\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\mathsf E u=e,\qquad \mathsf G_u u\le g.
\label{eq:standardqp}
\end{equation}
The dimensions are $u\in\R^{n_\chi}$, $\mathsf E\in\R^{n_h\times n_\chi}$, and $\mathsf G_u\in\R^{n_q\times n_\chi}$ with $n_q=n_\chi+n_g$. Let $\lambda^{=}$ be the unrestricted equality multiplier and $\lambda^{\le}\ge0$ the inequality multiplier. The dual value at any dual-feasible pair is
\begin{equation}
d_{\rm QP}(\lambda^{=},\lambda^{\le})=
-\tfrac12\|\mathsf E^\top\lambda^{=}+\mathsf G_u^\top\lambda^{\le}\|_2^2
-e^\top\lambda^{=}-g^\top\lambda^{\le}.
\label{eq:dualqp}
\end{equation}
For a primal-feasible $u$, weak duality makes the primal--dual gap non-negative. Using $\mathsf Eu=e$, the gap is the sum of two non-negative quantities given by
\begin{align}
\Gamma={}&\tfrac12u^\top u-d_{\rm QP}\notag\\
={}&\tfrac12\|u+\mathsf E^\top\lambda^{=}+\mathsf G_u^\top\lambda^{\le}\|_2^2
+(\lambda^{\le})^\top(g-\mathsf G_u u)\ge0.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{align}
Consequently, $\Gamma=0$ is equivalent to stationarity and complementarity and certifies that $u$ is the unique lower-QP solution. This strong-duality construction removes the $n_q$ explicit slack variables used by a conventional complementarity lift \citep{Boyd2004}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:gap} is used to audit the lower problem rather than added as a constraint to the operating search. This keeps the search in the operating coordinates instead of enlarging it with projection states and multipliers. The case study uses $d_\vartheta=7$ normalized controls. At each distinct trial, Equation~\eqref{eq:standardqp} is solved without a warm start using a convex-QP method \citep{Stellato2020}.

Gradient-based search also requires the effect of operating changes on the projected response. The active set comprises the inequality constraints that hold at equality at the solution. When this set is stable, total derivatives follow by differentiating the equality-plus-active-inequality Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) system. If these derivatives are unavailable, the deterministic Constrained Optimization BY Quadratic Approximations (COBYQA) method uses function values instead. Each distinct fallback trial receives a new cold solve of the same QP. The fallback therefore changes the search method, not the physical projection. Every upper constraint uses a fixed positive scale determined from development data, so scaling does not change feasibility. The calculation starts only from the deterministic box center and independently re-evaluates the selected endpoint.

Active-set changes can make derivative-based convergence claims unreliable. The calculation therefore distinguishes two levels of evidence. The strong tier requires the derivative audit, an independent lower active-set audit, and the seven-control upper KKT audit. If that tier is unavailable, protocol v3 evaluates neighboring feasible controls at normalized radii $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$. The coarse poll contains the 14 signed coordinate directions. The fine poll contains those axes, 84 signed pairwise diagonals, and eight Helmert-simplex directions. Coupled directions allow simultaneous control changes that a coordinate-only poll may miss near an operating limit. After an improving point is selected, search-only ray trials use multipliers $2,4,\ldots$ along the same direction, with at most 16 probes and unchanged inner-problem audits. These trials accelerate the search but do not provide convergence evidence. The accepted endpoint must still pass fresh complete polls.

Each complete poll must include at least one feasible nonzero displacement. Feasible-direction rank remains a diagnostic rather than an acceptance gate because active constraints can restrict the feasible cone to fewer than seven dimensions. An accepted fine-scale move triggers coarse-scale revalidation. Absence of sufficient feasible decrease in both revalidated polls provides evidence only for the tested directions and resolutions. It establishes neither KKT or Clarke stationarity nor a local or global optimum. An incomplete, failed, or evaluation-limited poll leaves stationarity unresolved.

\subsection{Direct smooth mechanistic comparator}

A direct mechanistic comparator is needed to assess whether surrogate-based decisions justify their prediction error and computational cost. Route $M$ removes regression and projection and solves the reactor and Clarifier balances within the optimization problem. Its state contains the concentrations that accumulate in the reduced mechanistic model as
\begin{equation}
y=\col(c_1,\ldots,c_N,s_1,\ldots,s_L)\in\R_+^{n_y},
\qquad n_y=NF+L.
\label{eq:directstate}
\end{equation}
The mixer and Clarifier outlet vectors are reconstructed algebraically from $(\vartheta,x,y)$. The common objective and constraints use the reduced response with $M_{\rm cl}=\sum_{\ell=1}^L V_{{\rm cl},\ell}s_\ell$. This mapping allows both routes to evaluate the same engineering quantities despite their different state dimensions.

Outlet reconstruction divides by the Clarifier-feed TSS, which could otherwise reach zero at an infeasible solver iterate. An auxiliary variable $\widetilde X_F\ge X_F^{\min}>0$ supplies a positive denominator, with the equality $\widetilde X_F=t_X^\top c_N$ enforced at feasibility. A differentiable residual $f_{\rm sm}(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F)$ contains all $NF$ reactor balances and all $L$ finite-volume Clarifier balances. The direct route also retains the full layer envelope underlying the surrogate inventory bounds. Define $g_{\rm env}(y)=\col(s_1\mathbf1_{L-2}-s_{2:L-1},s_{2:L-1}-s_L\mathbf1_{L-2})$. The auxiliary lower bound already imposes the feed-TSS engineering limit, so $g_{\rm eng,red}$ omits that duplicate row from $g_{\rm eng}$. The resulting nonlinear program (NLP) is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\vartheta,y,\widetilde X_F}\quad&J[\vartheta,\chi(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F);\varpi]\\
\text{subject to}\quad& f_{\rm sm}(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F)=0,\\
&\widetilde X_F=t_X^\top c_N,\quad \widetilde X_F\ge X_F^{\min},\\
&\vartheta\in\mathcal V,\quad y\ge0,\\
&g_{\rm env}(y)\le0,\\
&g_{\rm eng,red}[\vartheta,\chi(\vartheta,x,y,\widetilde X_F)]\le0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mechanisticnlp}
\end{equation}
Its selected feasible incumbent, when one exists, is denoted $(\vartheta_M,y_M,\widetilde X_{F,M})$. The auxiliary does not change a feasible solution but keeps reconstruction defined during the search.

Smooth approximations allow derivative-based optimization through the switches in the kinetic and settling equations. They are used only for minimum, maximum, positive-part, zero-denominator, and receiver-switch operations. A nonzero smoothing width changes the equations near a switch. The smooth endpoint must therefore be checked on the original nonsmooth model before its performance is compared with the surrogate-selected decision. Both routes use that same reference model for replay.

The reference model also has a defined physical scope. Its scalar-layer Clarifier tracks aggregate solids, and particulate outlet components are reconstructed with the steady feed composition. Time integration with a backward differentiation formula is used as a numerical route to the nonsmooth steady equations. It does not track the transient inventory of every particulate species in every layer. A reference replay is an independently converged solution of those nonsmoothed equations. Stability refers only to this reduced numerical state model.

\section{Methodology}

The evaluation separates three tasks corresponding to the research questions. Prediction assessment measures the effect of physical reconciliation. Mechanistic replay tests the operating decisions selected by each route. Paired objective and timing comparisons assess their practical trade-offs. The following procedures define the case study, data use, fitting, optimization, and numerical acceptance for these tasks.

\subsection{Case-study definition}

The case study provides a common treatment system on which to compare the two optimization routes. Its topology, decision bounds, objective priorities, and engineering limits are specified here rather than treated as requirements of the general formulation. Kinetic, settling, and numerical constants are tabulated in the Supplementary Material. The case uses Activated Sludge Model No. 2d with two-step nitrification (ASM2d-TSN), with $F=20$ components, $F_S=F_X=10$, and $K=5$ named invariants. Five equal-volume reactors are followed by a ten-layer Clarifier. Reactors 1 and 2 are unaerated, and reactors 3--5 have independent aeration controls. Thus
\begin{equation}
\vartheta=(H,a_3,a_4,a_5,r_I,r_R,w)^\top\in\R^7,
\label{eq:casecontrols}
\end{equation}
while $h_i=H/5$, $a_1=a_2=0$, $k_La_i=47a_i$ d$^{-1}$ for $i=3,4,5$, and $\xi_i=0$ for every stage.

Five equal-volume stages provide a compact anoxic--aerobic train for the comparative calculation. Ten 0.4-m Clarifier layers resolve the specified 4-m depth without making the layer count a structural constant. The operating box spans low-to-high residence time, aeration, recycle, and wasting settings while preserving $q_E>0$ and $q_U>0$. These choices define the case-study domain and are not universal design bounds.

For this case, extended ICSOR has $d_z=d_\vartheta+F=27$ and $n_{\phi,S}=406$. It assembles $n_\chi=161$ responses with $n_h=76$, $n_g=12$, and $n_q=173$, while the mechanistic route has $n_y=110$. The scalar log-regression overflow closure adds one equality but no response coordinate or inequality. These dimensions follow from Equations~\eqref{eq:state}, \eqref{eq:featurecount}, \eqref{eq:eqcount}, and \eqref{eq:G}. They are not fixed architectural constants. Changing $N$ or the free-control set changes the surrogate problem and requires renewed validation. Changing $L$ changes $n_y$, the mechanistic discretization, and the construction of $M_{\rm cl}$, but not $n_\chi$. Mechanistic and response validation must still be repeated.

The objective gives the largest individual weight to effluent quality while retaining explicit penalties for resource use. Its priorities are
\begin{equation}
\varpi=(0.50,0.15,0.20,0.05,0.05,0.05)^\top,
\qquad \varpi_Q=\tfrac14\mathbf1_4.
\label{eq:caseweights}
\end{equation}
Aeration receives the largest resource coefficient because oxygen transfer is an important energy demand in the treatment train. HRT represents installed biological volume. The remaining resource coefficients apply to MLR, RAS, and wasted-solids production. The normalized objective components are not all confined to the same interval, so the coefficients are trade-off parameters rather than percentages of the attained objective. These values define this comparative case only. $D_T$ contains the positive development-set standard deviations of effluent COD, TN, TP, and TSS. The fixed aeration normalization is $E_A^{\rm ref}=\sum_{i=3}^{5}V_i(H^U)(47\,\mathrm{d}^{-1})=423{,}000$ m$^3$ d$^{-1}$, the maximum transfer-capacity proxy in the case box.

The case imposes $\mathrm{SRT}\le30$ d, $\mathrm{SLR}\le100$ kg TSS m$^{-2}$ d$^{-1}$, $X_U\le15{,}000$ g TSS m$^{-3}$, $X_N\ge1$ g TSS m$^{-3}$, and $\dot M_X/Q_0\ge1$ g TSS m$^{-3}$. It imposes no minimum SRT. SRT is calculated and reported for every finite replay, but a value below 8 d does not by itself exclude a decision from optimization or paired comparison. The wasted-solids objective uses $X_U^{\rm ref}=15{,}000$ g TSS m$^{-3}$. SOR is reported but not constrained during optimization. The declared $Q_0$, $A_{\rm cl}$, and $w$ range imply $6.33\le\mathrm{SOR}\le6.66$ m d$^{-1}$, which already satisfies a 20 m d$^{-1}$ upper limit. These are case choices, not universal activated sludge limits. Influent ranges, Clarifier settling constants, kinetic parameters, and numerical tolerances are listed in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Study sequence and protection against information leakage}

Separating data generation, fitting, assessment, and optimization limits the use of assessment outcomes to tune the model. After the protocol revisions documented below, the calculation follows six fixed phases.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Retain all completed deterministic candidate attempts over the joint seven-control and twenty-influent domain, including accepted and rejected outcomes. Generate no further supplemental candidates.
\item Freeze the 16,714 accepted mechanistic states and retain their partition into 13,371 development states and 3,343 post-selection holdout states in ascending original candidate order.
\item Construct the reduced plant response from the stored states. Select the ridge setting by plant-grouped five-fold cross-validation (CV) within the development block and fit the final surrogate.
\item Evaluate the raw and projected surrogate responses once on the post-selection holdout for descriptive prediction assessment.
\item Attempt the nominal and ten influent cases using both optimization routes. Apply the appropriate surrogate convergence checks and retain every success or failure status. Permit the stated route-$S$ conditional recovery only after a failed primary direct solve.
\item Replay every available selected decision on the same exact nonsmooth mechanistic model and perform the common-reference and physical audits.
\end{enumerate}
Within the revised analysis, holdout and optimization responses do not determine feature definitions, scales, ridge selection, objective priorities, limits, smoothing widths, tolerances, or starts. However, earlier assessment outcomes informed the response architecture and later protocol revisions. In particular, layer-wise summaries had been inspected before the 25 August 2026 reduction to $M_{\rm cl}$. The 3,343 rows are therefore a post-selection holdout rather than an untouched or confirmatory test set.

An earlier implementation check used 400 development inputs, 100 untouched test inputs, five fresh influent scenarios, and a five-layer Clarifier. The independent seeds were 500042, 500043, and 500314159, respectively. This check could continue after a scientific acceptance failure to exercise checkpointing, failure accounting, optimization, and reporting. Its fitted quantities and results are excluded from article-scale analysis. It used the box-center start instead of the then-planned nine-start search and imposed a 600 s limit on each embedded-surrogate Interior Point OPTimizer (IPOPT) continuation stage. This historical check does not define the current surrogate solver. The article-scale workflow also records failed scientific gates and attempts the remaining calculations without refitting or changing thresholds. Such continuation tests the implementation. Only a run that passes every frozen scientific gate can support the article's scientific conclusions.

On 23 August 2026, before article-scale generation or response inspection, the planned workload increased from 800 development and 200 test inputs to 4,000 development and 1,000 test inputs. This prospective revision changed the sample counts and their dependent denominators. It retained the generators, seeds, 80/20 partition, model form, gates, ten scenarios, ten-layer geometry, and then-planned nine-start optimization. The completed 500-input implementation check remained separate.

On 24 August 2026, after the 5,000-input workflow was completed, a new run targeted 50,000 accepted states with a 40,000/10,000 development/test split and ten influent cases. It retained the 80/20 partition, generators, seeds, replacement policy, model, gates, ten-layer geometry, and revised single-start and casewise validation protocols. Changing the Latin-hypercube sample count changes the design itself. The new run therefore used a separate result directory and did not reuse the previous 5,000 states.

Generation was stopped later on 24 August 2026, and the available accepted checkpoints were frozen for analysis. Of 18,211 completed attempts, 16,714 passed the full two-start acceptance checks and 1,497 were rejected. No further replacement generation is performed. Accepted candidates are ordered by original candidate index and partitioned before fitting into 13,371 development rows and 3,343 holdout rows. This workload reduction occurred after generation outcomes were available. The holdout is a deterministic partition of the interrupted development stream, not the originally planned independent test stream. These limitations restrict the interpretation of its assessment results.

The replacement policy had changed on 23 August 2026 after the initial 4,000-candidate development round was audited, but before fitting, test assessment, or optimization. A candidate-level failure no longer terminated generation. Each rejected attempt and its diagnostics remained in an immutable ledger, and a deterministic supplemental candidate was evaluated in its place. Accepted checkpoints were retained after verification of their calculation settings and content hashes. This change was informed by observed generation failures rather than specified prospectively. Replacement ceased when the accepted set was frozen on 24 August.

During the resumed calculation on 23 August, a development out-of-fold projection exposed premature termination of the trust-region-reflective bounded least-squares routine used to reconstruct projection multipliers. The primal QP had residuals near roundoff, so the failure was confined to the independent dual reconstruction. A deterministic bounded-variable least-squares active-set algorithm replaced that routine, as described in the Supplementary Material. The QP, data, ridge fit, and acceptance tolerances were unchanged. The run record retains this correction and all failed scientific gates. Continuing execution does not convert a failed gate into a pass.

The approximately 450-variable embedded-KKT surrogate NLP and its seven continuation stages proved computationally impractical for the former layer-output response. On 23 August, that solver and the nine-start search were replaced by a single box-center start for each route and case. The surrogate then used the seven-variable exact-QP active-set calculation, with COBYQA when exact derivatives were unavailable. Both search methods evaluated the unchanged cold projection QP. The direct route retained its three-stage smoothing continuation. This revision was informed by observed computational cost and limited exploration to one local basin. The earlier fit, failures, budget-limited candidates, and unresolved audits remain archived but are excluded after the response revision described below.

On 24 August, preliminary case results exposed unresolved surrogate stationarity and sensitivity to branch-boundary classification. The comparison protocol was revised after these outcomes were inspected. Whole-holdout smooth/reference equivalence was removed as an admission test, and partial legacy results were archived and excluded. Each available selected decision was instead replayed from both declared starts on the common nonsmooth reference. A branch-boundary ambiguity qualified the interpretation but did not alone reject a physically valid, reproducible result. \subsection{Plant-grouped five-fold cross-validation and final surrogate fits}

All unit responses from one simulated plant share the same operating conditions and must remain together during model assessment. Plant-grouped fitting prevents information from one plant state appearing in both fitting and validation subsets. The 13,371 development rows are partitioned reproducibly into five folds whose sizes differ by at most one. For a fitting index set $\mathcal I$, let $\Phi_{\mathcal I}$ contain feature rows and $\widetilde Y_{\mathcal I}$ contain standardized responses. The matrix $R_\phi=\diag(0,1,\ldots,1)$ leaves the intercept unpenalized. The raw surrogate fit solves
\begin{equation}
\widehat C_{S,\gamma_S}=\argminop_C
\left\{\frac{\|\widetilde Y_{\mathcal I}-\Phi_{\mathcal I}C^\top\|_F^2}
{|\mathcal I|n_\chi}+\frac{\gamma_S}{n_\chi}\|CR_\phi\|_F^2\right\}.
\label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
Every center and scale is recomputed within the fitting fold to avoid using validation information in preprocessing. Positive candidate values of $\gamma_S$ give a unique coefficient solution and improve conditioning when features are nearly dependent. The fold score is the standardized root mean squared error (RMSE) of the full raw response. To favor stronger regularization when predictive performance is similar, the one-standard-error rule chooses the largest $\gamma_S$ whose mean score is within one standard error of the minimum \citep{Hastie2009}. Projection metrics are recorded but do not select $\gamma_S$. This separation prevents reconciliation from concealing a weak raw fit during model selection. Replacing ten layers by one inventory changes response weighting, so ridge selection and out-of-fold predictions are recomputed. The selected model is refitted once on all 13,371 development rows.

The scalar closure uses targets $\ell_{E,i}=\log(X_{E,i}/X_\star)$ and the same fold-specific second-order feature construction. For fitting set $\mathcal I$, it solves
\begin{equation}
\widehat\beta_{E,\gamma_E}=\argminop_\beta
\left\{\frac{1}{|\mathcal I|}\|\ell_{E,\mathcal I}-\Phi_{\mathcal I}\beta\|_2^2
+\gamma_E\|R_\phi\beta\|_2^2\right\}.
\label{eq:logridge}
\end{equation}
The penalty grid, grouped folds, fold-local scaling, one-standard-error rule, and final development-only refit follow the multiresponse protocol. Each development row is projected with a value of $\widehat X_{E,\log}$ fitted without that row's fold. This avoids evaluating the empirical closure only on data used to fit it. The final refit is used for holdout, optimization, and selected-decision evaluations. Integrity checks require positive mechanistic training targets, finite log predictions and back-transformations, and verified hashes of the closure coefficients and preprocessing state.

Whole-plant averages can hide poor predictions at a particular unit or outlet. The post-selection holdout therefore reports descriptive RMSE, mean absolute error (MAE), bias, normalized root mean squared error (nRMSE), and coefficient of determination ($R^2$) by response block and for the complete response. The blocks cover the mixer, each reactor, both outlet flows, and Clarifier inventory. Raw and projected predictions are reported together to show the effect of reconciliation. Overflow TSS is assessed on concentration and logarithmic scales, with low-concentration and upper-tail groups fixed from development quantiles. This separates accuracy during low-solids operation from errors in high-solids breakthrough cases.

The empirical closure can place a mechanistic target outside the projection set even when that target satisfies the physical balances. Its distance to the set must therefore be included when interpreting projection error. For holdout row $i$, let $\mathcal C_{\log,i}$ be the projection polyhedron containing Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure}. Let $P_{\mathcal C_{\log,i}}$ denote metric projection in the norm $\|v\|_{D_\chi^{-1}}=\|D_\chi^{-1}v\|_2$. The reported distance is $\delta_{{\rm feas},i}=\|P_{\mathcal C_{\log,i}}(\chi_i)-\chi_i\|_{D_\chi^{-1}}$, where $\chi_i$ is the numerical mechanistic target. This distance also accounts for finite numerical feasibility tolerances. The general error bound checked for every target is
\begin{equation}
\|P_{\mathcal C_{\log,i}}(\chi_{{\rm raw},S,i})-\chi_i\|_{D_\chi^{-1}}
\le \|\chi_{{\rm raw},S,i}-\chi_i\|_{D_\chi^{-1}}+\delta_{{\rm feas},i}.
\label{eq:finitefeasibility}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:nonexpansive} applies only to an exactly feasible target. Satisfying every network identity is insufficient if Equation~\eqref{eq:overflowclosure} is not also satisfied. Scientific admission gates require all development out-of-fold projections to be feasible and to pass primal--dual audits. The raw nRMSE must remain below one for both the full response and the inventory coordinate. All trust limits must be fixed, and concentration-scale and log-scale overflow-TSS errors must be reported, before optimization. Separate inventory and overflow checks prevent these decision-relevant quantities from being hidden in the 161-coordinate average. Descriptive holdout metrics do not alter these gates, and a failed gate does not trigger refitting. Execution stops when a required numerical object is non-finite or incomplete and the downstream problem cannot be defined.

\subsection{Nominal optimization and exact common-reference replay}

A common operating problem is needed to distinguish differences between models from differences in decision freedom. The nominal influent is the midpoint of each declared influent interval. Both routes use the same free controls, bounds, objective priorities, and engineering constraints and begin at the center of the normalized seven-dimensional box. Extended ICSOR uses the exact-QP active-set calculation in the seven controls. The former primal--dual-gap IPOPT continuation is not executed.

The system-wide projection QP is cold-solved to its acceptance tolerances at every distinct surrogate trial. On a stable active set, response derivatives follow from the QP KKT sensitivity system after rank and conditioning checks. The derivative chain includes $q_E\widehat X_{E,\log}$ through the quadratic features and exponential back-transformation. An active-set change triggers a fresh QP and derivative reconstruction. If these derivatives cannot be constructed, deterministic value-only COBYQA continues within the normalized bounds. Each distinct fallback trial receives a new cold solve and complete audit of the same QP. The fallback uses neither an approximate projection nor finite-difference derivatives. Its best feasible visited point is independently cold-projected again.

An extended-ICSOR candidate receives the strong first-order tier only if its derivative chain and independent lower-QP and upper KKT audits pass. Otherwise, complete feasible no-descent polls at normalized radii $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ provide only finite-resolution evidence under protocol v3. After an improving poll step, up to 16 search-only evaluations expand geometrically along that ray. Every accepted move requires a new complete poll, so acceleration trials cannot supply convergence evidence. Each completed poll must contain a feasible nonzero displacement. Direction rank is diagnostic only. An accepted fine-scale move requires another coarse poll, and certification has a 10,000-evaluation safety budget. A failed or incomplete poll leaves stationarity unresolved. First-order stationarity does not establish local minimality. A finite poll establishes neither KKT or Clarke stationarity nor local or global optimality.

The primary direct solve uses the three-stage smoothing continuation from the box center and a full-space endpoint audit. If it fails, one recovery may start from the same case's certified surrogate control using the unchanged sequence. No recovery follows a successful primary attempt or an uncertified surrogate candidate. This recovery may provide a usable direct candidate, but it depends on route $S$ and is not independent basin exploration. A validated feasible point remains reportable when its search budget is exhausted or its stationarity is unresolved. If no validated candidate is obtained, the failure remains in the case denominator and execution proceeds.

Comparing each route's predicted objective alone would mix decision quality with surrogate error or smoothing error. Every available selected candidate $k\in\{S,M\}$ is therefore replayed on the exact nonsmooth mechanistic model. The full layer state is mapped to the common reduced response $\chi_{{\rm ref},k}$ using Equations~\eqref{eq:state} and \eqref{eq:inventory}. The responses used within optimization are $\chi_{{\rm nat},S}=\widehat\chi_S$ and $\chi_{{\rm nat},M}=\chi_{{\rm sm},M}$. Extended ICSOR is also evaluated, wherever defined, at both selected controls. This permits prediction error to be assessed at a fixed decision and decision quality to be assessed on a fixed reference. For $k\in\{S,M\}$, define
\begin{align}
e_{{\rm raw},S|k}&=D_\chi^{-1}[\chi_{{\rm raw},S}(\vartheta_k,x)-\chi_{{\rm ref},k}],\notag\\
e_{{\rm proj},S|k}&=D_\chi^{-1}[\widehat\chi_S(\vartheta_k,x)-\chi_{{\rm ref},k}],\notag\\
e_{\rm nat,k}&=D_\chi^{-1}[\chi_{{\rm nat},k}-\chi_{{\rm ref},k}],\notag\\
\Delta J_{{\rm nat-ref},k}&=J(\vartheta_k,\chi_{{\rm nat},k})-J(\vartheta_k,\chi_{{\rm ref},k}),\notag\\
\Delta J_{S-M}&=J(\vartheta_S,\chi_{{\rm ref},S})-J(\vartheta_M,\chi_{{\rm ref},M}).
\label{eq:comparisons}
\end{align}
Each valid, physically audited replay retains its objective and objective components even when an engineering safeguard is violated. Such a candidate is reported but excluded from paired comparison. The objective difference in Equation~\eqref{eq:comparisons} is reported only when both candidates are available and feasible within their respective optimization models. Both exact replays must also be valid, non-negative, compliant with the layer envelope, and feasible under the retained engineering safeguards. These safeguards cover maximum SRT, SLR, underflow TSS, feed TSS, and external solids loss. No minimum SRT is imposed, so a replay below 8 d remains eligible if every retained check passes. The differences compare selected feasible candidates and are not termed regret because neither route has a global optimality certificate. A symmetric relative objective difference and normalized control root mean square (RMS) and maximum differences accompany each eligible pair.

Two declared reference initializations check whether replay converges reproducibly at each selected decision. Their generation-scale and frozen-state-scale root discrepancies must each be at most $10^{-6}$. Branch assignments must agree except within a declared boundary-ambiguity band. A mismatch in that band qualifies the result but does not alone reject an otherwise agreeing root. Mass-conservation, non-negativity, layer-residual, domain, and reduced-stability audits must also pass. Near a branch boundary, differentiable stationarity or smooth/reference equivalence cannot be claimed without qualification. Whole-holdout smooth/reference validation is no longer used. The 3,343 holdout inputs support only descriptive raw and projected prediction assessment, and partial legacy equivalence results are excluded.

\subsection{Timing and numerical acceptance}

Fast regression evaluation does not necessarily imply fast optimization once projection, convergence checks, and reference replay are included. Timing therefore separates regression, projection, complete surrogate optimization and certification, primary direct optimization, conditional direct recovery, and exact replay. These records show whether a shorter run reflects computational efficiency or an incomplete calculation. A solver success flag alone is insufficient. \section{Conclusions}

This study formulates a plant-wide extension of ICSOR and a direct smooth mechanistic comparator for the same activated sludge system. The objective is to assess whether physical reconciliation can support useful operating decisions at a lower repeated optimization cost. Extended ICSOR predicts the connected unit responses and reconciles them with network balances, conserved inventories, and non-negativity. Outlet component flows close the recycle, and the aggregate Clarifier inventory supplies the storage term needed for whole-plant SRT. A separate log regression provides a positive overflow-TSS closure. The surrogate does not predict internal Clarifier layers.

The projection makes the smallest standardized correction satisfying the declared physical constraints, empirical overflow equality, and outlet-derived inventory bounds. These requirements prevent specific inconsistencies but do not enforce all kinetic and settling equations. Numerical audits likewise distinguish a correctly solved projection from convergence of the operating search. Derivative-based first-order evidence and finite-resolution feasible polls are reported separately, and neither establishes global optimality.

The evaluation addresses prediction accuracy, the validity of selected operating decisions, and the trade-off between decision quality and computational cost as separate questions. Both routes share the controls, objective, engineering limits, and exact nonsmooth reference. Replay checks nonlinear kinetics, all ten Clarifier layer balances, stability, and engineering feasibility at every available selected decision. The 3,343-row holdout and ten influent scenarios provide descriptive post-selection evidence rather than independent confirmation. Numerical answers to the research questions remain pending completion of the required result tables. The present formulation does not establish an accuracy or speed advantage before those results are available.
