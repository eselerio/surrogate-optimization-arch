\section*{Abstract}

\begin{singlepar}
    Biological wastewater treatment relies on activated sludge processes whose behavior is governed by
    interacting carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, oxygen, biomass, and solids transformations. Mechanistic models
    can represent these interactions, but repeated simulation can be burdensome when many operating and
    influent conditions must be examined. This research develops a surrogate modeling framework for activated
    sludge prediction that combines machine learning accuracy, physical admissibility, and interpretability.
    The study first evaluates whether modern tabular learning models can approximate the complete component
    response of a mechanistic activated sludge simulator from paired input and output data. It then examines
    how post prediction enforcement of conservation and nonnegativity affects surrogate outputs, and it
    considers how interpretable constrained regression can support engineering judgment. The work is scoped to
    simulated steady-state data under a defined activated sludge component representation. Within that scope,
    the framework clarifies how fast data driven prediction can be evaluated alongside the physical meaning of
    the returned component state.
\end{singlepar}

\paragraph*{Keywords.} activated sludge process; machine learning surrogate; wastewater treatment; physical
admissibility; interpretable modeling

\section{Review of Related Literature}

\subsection{Water Pollution, Biological Treatment, and Activated Sludge Modeling}

Water pollution is a public health and environmental problem whose consequences depend on pollutant sources,
exposure pathways, and the vulnerability of affected populations. \citet{Lin2022} connect polluted water to
heterogeneous disease burdens, while \citet{Madhav2019} organize water pollutants by origin and effect across
industrial, agricultural, domestic, and natural pathways. These reviews establish the wider environmental
motivation for engineered treatment systems. Wastewater treatment is not only a matter of meeting a numerical
effluent target. It is part of the larger effort to interrupt pollutant movement from human activity into
aquatic ecosystems and exposed communities.

The activated sludge process remains a central biological route for municipal and industrial wastewater
treatment because it brings microbial growth, organic carbon removal, nitrogen conversion, phosphorus behavior,
oxygen transfer, and solids production into one engineered system. The process is complex because its treatment
performance arises from interacting microbial and physicochemical pathways rather than from a single dominant
reaction. \citet{Duan2022} show this clearly in their review of heterotrophic nitrifying bacteria, where
nitrogen removal depends on microbial pathways whose importance changes with wastewater composition and
operating conditions. \citet{Wentzel1992} likewise present nitrification, denitrification, and biological
excess phosphorus removal as coupled activated sludge processes. Their review helps explain why carbon,
nitrogen, phosphorus, biomass, and solids must be treated as interdependent states when the process is modeled.

The Activated Sludge Model (ASM) family gives this interdependence a mechanistic language. \citet{Henze1999}
introduced ASM2d as a model for biological phosphorus removal with simultaneous nitrification and
denitrification, and \citet{Henze2006} consolidated the wider ASM family as a standard framework for design,
operation, teaching, and research. The relevance of ASM2d is especially strong for work that must represent
carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, alkalinity, and suspended solids together. Later developments also made explicit
the need to represent nitrite-producing and nitrite-consuming steps when biological nutrient removal is studied
in more detail. \citet{Guerrero2011} used an ASM2d-based formulation with two-step nitrification and
denitrification to examine competition between phosphorus accumulating organisms and denitrifiers. This
component-based tradition frames activated sludge prediction as a state prediction problem rather than only an
aggregate effluent estimation problem. The object of prediction is therefore not merely an aggregate effluent
quality index but a complete activated sludge state from which reported
composites can be derived.

\subsection{Mechanistic Simulation and the Need for Surrogates}

Mechanistic activated sludge models are valuable because they preserve the biological and chemical structure of
the process, but that same structure makes repeated simulation demanding. Nonlinear kinetics, interacting
operating variables, uncertain influent conditions, and calibration decisions all affect the behavior of a
simulated treatment system. \citet{Araujo2013} demonstrate this in their sensitivity analysis of optimal
operation and controlled variable selection for an activated sludge process model. Their work shows that an
operating decision depends on how model states respond across the process, not only on a single effluent
quantity. \citet{Machado2014} add a complementary calibration perspective by showing that ASM2d parameter
identifiability is entangled with influent and operational uncertainty in full-scale plant data.

The burden becomes more visible when a model is embedded in design or optimization. \citet{Aboagye2021}
formulate wastewater treatment network design as an optimization and sustainability assessment problem in
which many configurations must be evaluated. \citet{PadronPaez2020} similarly place plant design within a
multiobjective framework that balances treatment performance and sustainability criteria. \citet{Fang2011}
show the same need in biological nutrient removal, where a simulation-based approach is used to optimize a
full-scale treatment process. These applications require a model to be queried many times while operating
points, treatment alternatives, and tradeoffs are explored. A detailed simulator can then become the limiting
element in a workflow whose value depends on broad search.

Surrogate modeling addresses this pressure by replacing repeated high-cost simulations with faster statistical
approximations over a defined operating domain. \citet{BhosekarIerapetritou2018} review surrogate modeling in
process systems and emphasize that sampling, validation, feasibility analysis, and optimization must be treated
as linked concerns. The wastewater literature shows the same practical motivation. \citet{Durkin2024} train
surrogates on high-fidelity wastewater resource recovery simulations and use them inside process optimization.
The significance of these studies is not simply that surrogate models run quickly. Their deeper importance is
that they allow mechanistic knowledge to be used repeatedly in design, screening, and decision support where a
full simulator would be inconvenient or too slow.

\subsection{Machine Learning Surrogates for Wastewater Prediction}

Data driven wastewater modeling has moved from plant monitoring toward operational prediction and decision
support. \citet{Newhart2019} review performance analyses of wastewater treatment plants and note both the
promise of process data and the difficulty of turning that data into reliable plant knowledge. More recent
studies show the field moving toward actionable prediction. \citet{Mao2024} use artificial intelligence tools
to balance effluent quality and aeration energy, \citet{Yang2025} use machine learning to predict waste
activated sludge generation for operational efficiency, and \citet{Wang2025} compare interpretable machine
learning models with a mechanistic model for effluent nitrogen prediction. \citet{Xiao2026} further illustrates
the growing interest in frameworks that combine mechanistic dynamics with data driven surrogates for activated
sludge management. This movement also extends to process operation and control, as shown by data-mining work
on aeration optimization, neural-network methods for setpoint and model predictive control, and reinforcement
learning reviews for wastewater treatment process control \citep{Asadi2017,Sadeghassadi2018,Croll2023}.

The surrogate families used in this area differ in what they assume about the process. Partial least squares is
a natural baseline because it creates a compact multivariate relationship among correlated variables.
\citet{Wold2001} describe partial least squares regression as a basic chemometric tool, and \citet{Khatri2020}
review its use in water quality analysis. Its appeal is interpretability, especially when influent and
operating variables are strongly correlated. Its limitation is that strongly nonlinear microbial behavior may
not be captured without richer features or nonlinear extensions. \citet{Woo2009} show the value of kernel
partial least squares for online estimation in an industrial cokes wastewater treatment plant, and
\citet{Yang2021} combine partial least squares with a relevance vector machine for adaptive effluent quality
prediction. These studies show how linear interpretability and nonlinear predictive flexibility have both
been pursued in wastewater modeling.

Tree ensembles, boosting methods, and neural networks offer different ways of handling nonlinear tabular
relationships. \citet{Breiman2001} introduced random forests as ensembles of randomized trees, while
\citet{ChenGuestrin2016} developed XGBoost as a scalable boosted tree system for structured data. Neural
approaches provide another form of flexibility. \citet{Hornik1989} established the approximation capacity of
multilayer feedforward networks, and \citet{ArikPfister2021} introduced TabNet as a deep learning model for
tabular data with attentive feature selection. In activated sludge surrogate modeling, these algorithm families
are not interchangeable labels. They represent different compromises among interpretability, data efficiency,
nonlinear response learning, computational cost, and stability outside the center of the training domain.

\subsection{Physical Admissibility Beyond Predictive Accuracy}

A central limitation of an unconstrained surrogate is that predictive accuracy does not by itself establish
physical admissibility. A surrogate can be close to a mechanistic target under ordinary error metrics while
still returning component concentrations that cannot be interpreted as a physically valid activated sludge
state. The ASM tradition represents process transformations through stoichiometric relations among component
states \citep{Henze1999,Henze2006}. Within a fixed control volume and model boundary, those relations imply
material balances that the influent and effluent component states should respect. Concentrations must also
remain nonnegative if they are to retain their physical meaning.

This distinction matters because reported composites such as chemical oxygen demand, total nitrogen, total
phosphorus, and total suspended solids are summaries of an underlying component state. An aggregate value can
look plausible while the component vector behind it contains negative concentrations or violates a declared
material balance. For wastewater decision support, that mismatch is not a harmless numerical detail. It can
distort interpretation, affect downstream optimization, and make a prediction less credible even when the
aggregate error appears small.

The broader scientific machine learning literature reinforces this concern. \citet{Kashinath2021} review
physics informed case studies in weather and climate modeling and show that scientific prediction must be
judged against physical structure as well as empirical error. \citet{Hansen2024} focus on conservation laws
and argue that learned physical models may need explicit mechanisms to respect conserved quantities.
\citet{SturmWexler2020} and \citet{SturmWexler2022} demonstrate the issue in photochemical modeling, where
machine learning speedups must preserve mass and atom balances to remain credible. \citet{Shen2023} further
connect data driven prediction with physical modeling by presenting differentiable modeling as a route for
combining learned components with process-based structure. \citet{KircherVotsmeier2025}
make the paired requirement especially clear by showing that a conservative correction is incomplete when it
creates negative species and that a positivity preserving adjustment is incomplete when it breaks atom balance.
Although these studies arise outside activated sludge modeling, they identify the same conceptual problem that
appears when a wastewater surrogate predicts a full component state.

\subsection{Constraint Enforcement in Scientific Machine Learning}

Scientific machine learning has developed several strategies for incorporating physical structure into
data driven models. Physics informed approaches place governing relations, boundary conditions, or other
physical penalties inside the training objective. \citet{Raissi2019} formalize physics informed neural
networks for forward and inverse problems governed by differential equations, and \citet{Karniadakis2021}
place such models within a broader physics informed learning program. \citet{Shen2023} similarly emphasize
the value of connecting machine learning with process-based structure rather than treating the two as
separate paradigms. These methods are important because they make physical knowledge visible during training.
Their limitation for the present wastewater setting is that a penalty can encourage a physically reasonable
prediction without guaranteeing that every deployed prediction satisfies the required balance and
nonnegativity conditions.

Hard enforcement methods respond by building the physical requirement into the architecture, output
representation, or correction step. \citet{Beucler2021} show that analytic constraints can be enforced in
neural networks that emulate physical systems. \citet{Hansen2024} develop approaches for learning physical
models that can respect conservation laws. \citet{SturmWexler2020} and \citet{SturmWexler2022} use
conservative formulations for photochemical surrogates, while \citet{SturmSilva2024} extend the conservation
view through a species weighted correction for atmospheric composition. \citet{Valente2025} present output
projection onto physical manifolds as a way to return model predictions to physically consistent sets. These
works show that exact consistency can be imposed after, within, or around a learned model.

The activated sludge problem requires joint attention to conservation and nonnegativity. A conservation-only
method can still produce negative concentrations, while a positivity-only transformation can disturb the
case-specific balance implied by the mechanistic model. \citet{KircherVotsmeier2025} address this combined
requirement for mechanistic kinetics surrogates by embedding atom balance and positivity together. Their
application domains differ from wastewater treatment, but the central physical demand is parallel. A corrected
prediction must remain on the admissible balance relation while staying inside the domain of nonnegative
concentrations.

\subsection{Projection, Interpretability, and Dissertation Gap}

Post prediction projection is attractive for activated sludge surrogates because it can be applied after a
surrogate has already produced a component prediction. This makes the correction conceptually separate from the
learning algorithm and allows the same physical check to be placed after linear, kernel, neighbor, ensemble,
boosting, or neural predictors. The literature on conservation and output projection suggests that such a step
can return predictions to a physically consistent set \citep{KircherVotsmeier2025,Valente2025}. At the same
time, projection is not a substitute for model selection. It can make a prediction admissible while changing
ordinary error metrics in ways that depend on the surrogate, the component, and the operating regime. A
physically enforced wastewater surrogate therefore still requires paired evaluation of predictive accuracy and
constraint compliance.

Interpretability forms a second requirement for engineering usefulness. \citet{Rudin2019} argues that
high-stakes decisions should prefer models that are interpretable by design when such models can achieve
sufficient performance. Wastewater treatment modeling is not identical to the decision settings emphasized in
that argument, but the engineering concern is similar. If a surrogate is used for environmental decision
support, its learned relationships should be inspectable whenever the modeling task permits. This concern links
the older appeal of partial least squares, the current interest in interpretable tabular learning, and the need
for structured physically admissible surrogates.

The resulting gap is specific. Activated sludge models provide the component-space structure needed to
represent carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, alkalinity, and solids transformations, but repeated mechanistic
simulation can be too costly for broad exploration. Machine learning surrogates can accelerate prediction and
have already shown value in wastewater treatment applications, but low error does not guarantee conservation,
nonnegativity, or interpretability. Addressing this gap requires integrating surrogate comparison, post
prediction physical enforcement, and interpretable constrained regression into one activated sludge modeling
program. The aim is a surrogate framework that is fast enough for repeated use, accurate enough for
engineering analysis, physically admissible at the component level, and transparent enough to support
engineering judgment.

\section{Statement of the Problem}

Activated sludge models provide a rigorous mechanistic basis for representing biological wastewater treatment,
but their use becomes burdensome when many operating or influent conditions must be evaluated. At the same
time, machine learning surrogates can accelerate prediction but may return component states that are accurate
under conventional error metrics while remaining physically inadmissible. The central problem is therefore to
determine how machine learning surrogates of the activated sludge process can be developed and evaluated so
that their predictions remain useful not only as fast approximations, but also as physically meaningful
component-space states.

The problem begins with the predictive modeling setting itself. Wastewater treatment applications increasingly
use data driven models for effluent quality prediction, operational support, and process optimization
\citep{Newhart2019,Mao2024,Wang2025,Yang2025}. However, the activated sludge process is not a simple
single-output regression problem. The state described by ASM2d-based models contains interacting carbon,
nitrogen, phosphorus, alkalinity, and solids components \citep{Henze1999,Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. A surrogate
that predicts only reported aggregate quantities may be useful for limited monitoring tasks, but it does not
directly preserve the component-space information needed to examine conservation, nonnegativity, and
downstream composition calculations. A rigorous assessment must therefore establish how a range of statistical
surrogates behave when they are asked to predict the complete activated sludge component state rather than only
an aggregate water quality summary.

The second part of the problem concerns physical admissibility. Conventional surrogate assessment emphasizes
prediction error, but a low error value does not guarantee that an activated sludge prediction satisfies the
material balance structure implied by the adopted mechanistic model. It also does not guarantee that every
component concentration remains nonnegative. This creates a gap between numerical accuracy and engineering
credibility. Studies in scientific machine learning show that conservation and positivity often require
explicit treatment rather than passive expectation from data fitting alone
\citep{Beucler2021,Hansen2024,KircherVotsmeier2025,SturmWexler2020,SturmWexler2022}. It is therefore
necessary to determine whether post prediction enforcement can consistently return activated sludge surrogate
outputs to the physically admissible component space, and whether that enforcement improves, worsens, or leaves
unchanged the usual measures of predictive performance.

The third part of the problem concerns interpretability. A physically corrected prediction can be useful for
screening and computation, but correction alone does not necessarily make the learned relationship between
operating conditions, influent composition, and effluent state understandable. For engineering decision support,
the structure of the surrogate matters because the prediction may inform operating judgment, design
exploration, or later optimization. The research therefore examines whether an interpretable surrogate can be
structured so that it retains transparent relationships among operating variables, influent loads, nonlinear
effects, and component coupling while still returning physically admissible predictions. In this sense, the
research problem is not merely to reduce prediction error. It is to integrate accuracy, computational speed,
physical admissibility, and interpretability within one activated sludge surrogate modeling framework.

\section{Significance of the Study}

This study is significant because it addresses a practical weakness in the use of machine learning for
biological wastewater treatment modeling. Wastewater treatment systems protect receiving waters and public
health by reducing pollutant loads before discharge, and their operation depends on reliable understanding of
process behavior \citep{Lin2022,Madhav2019}. As treatment modeling moves toward data driven prediction and
optimization, the credibility of a surrogate model cannot rest only on speed or ordinary error metrics
\citep{Asadi2017,Fang2011,Sadeghassadi2018,Croll2023}. A prediction that violates mass conservation or
contains negative component concentrations may be numerically close to a target but still unsuitable for
engineering interpretation \citep{Hansen2024,KircherVotsmeier2025,Shen2023}. By placing physical
admissibility at the same level of importance as accuracy, the study strengthens the basis on which surrogate
predictions can be used in wastewater treatment analysis.

The study contributes to activated sludge modeling by keeping the complete component state at the center of the
surrogate problem. The ASM family was developed to represent interacting biological and chemical processes,
not only final aggregate effluent indicators \citep{Henze1999,Henze2006}. A component-space surrogate retains
that mechanistic orientation while reducing the cost of repeated evaluation. This matters for sensitivity
analysis, design screening, operating exploration, and optimization workflows where many simulations may be
needed \citep{Araujo2013,Aboagye2021,PadronPaez2020,Durkin2024}. The work therefore helps connect the
mechanistic detail of activated sludge models with the computational convenience expected from modern
surrogate modeling.

The study also offers methodological value for wastewater applications of machine learning. It separates
ordinary predictive accuracy from physical constraint compliance and treats both as necessary diagnostics for
complete component predictions. This distinction is important because different surrogate families may show
different error behavior, different tendencies toward negative values, and different responses to physical
projection. The resulting evaluation logic can help future wastewater surrogate studies report not only
whether a model predicts well, but also whether its predictions remain admissible under the declared process
boundary and component basis.

Finally, the study is significant for engineering decision support because it addresses interpretability
alongside feasibility. Interpretable models can be preferable in high-consequence settings when their
performance is sufficient, since they allow the structure of the learned relationship to be inspected rather
than treated only as a black-box output \citep{Rudin2019}. In the activated sludge setting, interpretability
supports the examination of how operating conditions and influent loads are associated with the predicted
effluent state. A physically admissible and interpretable surrogate can therefore serve as a bridge between
mechanistic simulation and practical decision workflows, providing predictions that are faster than repeated
simulation, more physically defensible than unconstrained regression outputs, and more inspectable than purely
black-box approximations.

\section{Research Framework}

The research framework is organized as a Theory of Change. In evaluation and intervention
research, a Theory of Change makes explicit how a set of activities is expected to produce outputs, outcomes,
and longer-term value under stated assumptions. \citet{Mayne2015} emphasizes that useful Theory of Change
models clarify the causal logic of a program rather than merely listing its components. \citet{DeSilva2014}
and \citet{Breuer2016} similarly show that Theory of Change reasoning can strengthen the design and evaluation
of complex interventions by connecting context, mechanisms, evidence, and intended change. In this
research, the intervention is methodological rather than institutional. The intended change is a more
credible way of using machine learning surrogates for activated sludge modeling, where computational speed is
joined with component-level physical admissibility and interpretability.

The starting condition is the tension identified in the review of related literature. Mechanistic activated
sludge models encode the biological and chemical structure of wastewater treatment, but repeated simulation can
be too costly for design exploration, screening, and optimization. Machine learning surrogates address the
computational burden, but unconstrained predictions may violate conservation or nonnegativity even when ordinary
error metrics are favorable \citep{Hansen2024,KircherVotsmeier2025,Shen2023}. The Theory of Change therefore
begins from the premise that wastewater surrogate modeling should be evaluated not only by closeness to
simulated targets, but also by whether the returned component state remains physically meaningful under the
adopted ASM boundary.

The framework rests on the assumption that complete component-state prediction is more appropriate than direct
aggregate-only prediction when the purpose is to preserve activated sludge process structure. It also assumes
that physical admissibility must be checked after prediction because it is not guaranteed by low error alone,
and that engineering usefulness is strengthened when the learned relationships among operating conditions,
influent loads, and effluent components can be inspected. These assumptions shape three connected research
activities. The first activity establishes the surrogate prediction setting and compares statistical learners
for complete activated sludge component states. The second activity evaluates post prediction enforcement of
mass conservation and nonnegativity across surrogate families and data regimes. The third activity develops an
interpretable physically admissible surrogate that integrates structured regression with checked deployment.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

This study is scoped to machine learning surrogates of the activated sludge process under an
ASM2d-based component representation. The mechanistic foundation is the ASM family and its extension to
biological phosphorus removal and two-step nitrogen conversion \citep{Henze1999,Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. The
surrogate task is defined at the level of activated sludge component states rather than only at the level of
reported effluent composites. Chemical oxygen demand, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and total suspended
solids remain important for interpretation, but they are treated as derived reporting quantities rather than as
the only prediction targets.

The empirical scope is limited to simulated steady-state reactor data. The study does not claim direct
field validation on full-scale wastewater treatment plant measurements, and it does not attempt to represent
every operational disturbance, sensor error, seasonal shift, or unmodeled biological pathway that may occur in
practice. The simulated setting is nevertheless appropriate for the present purpose because the study
asks how surrogate predictions behave when the mechanistic reference state, component basis, and physical
constraint structure are known. This allows prediction error, mass conservation, nonnegativity, and correction
behavior to be examined without confounding them with measurement noise or uncertain plant instrumentation.

The modeling scope includes a comparative set of statistical surrogates and an interpretable constrained
surrogate developed in the study. The comparison is intended to represent major surrogate families
used for structured process data, including linear latent-variable methods, neighbor and kernel methods, tree
ensembles, boosting methods, and neural networks. It is not an exhaustive survey of every possible machine
learning architecture, every hyperparameter strategy, or every hybrid mechanistic data driven formulation. The
findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence within the evaluated surrogate set and benchmark protocol,
not as a universal ranking of all possible wastewater treatment predictors.

The physical guarantees considered here are deliberately specific. Conservation refers to the
stoichiometric invariant structure implied by the adopted activated sludge model and system boundary, while
nonnegativity refers to the component concentrations returned by the surrogate. These guarantees do not imply
complete kinetic realism, thermodynamic validity, microbial ecological correctness, or plant-wide mass closure
outside the declared boundary. A prediction can be physically admissible in the constrained component-space
sense and still require further scrutiny before being used for detailed plant design, regulatory reporting, or
closed-loop control.

The evaluation also has a bounded generalization claim. It examines performance within the
sampled operating and influent domain and under controlled excursions beyond that domain, but it does not prove
reliability for arbitrary influent compositions, reactor configurations, treatment trains, climates, or
operational policies. The beyond-domain evaluations are useful because they reveal how prediction error and
physical enforcement behave when inputs move outside the training envelope. They should not be read as proof
that the surrogates will remain accurate under every future plant condition.

Finally, the interpretability claim is limited to the structure of the surrogate and the inspectability of its
learned relationships. A transparent coefficient block or component coupling pattern can support engineering
interpretation, but it should not be treated as a causal statement about the activated sludge process without
additional experimental or mechanistic analysis. Within these boundaries, the study contributes a framework
for evaluating and constructing activated sludge surrogates whose predictions are fast, physically
admissible under the stated constraints, and more interpretable than unconstrained black-box outputs.


\chapter{Machine Learning Surrogates of the Activated Sludge Process}
\label{ch:surrogates}

\section{Rationale}

Activated sludge processes remain central to biological wastewater treatment because they link organic-matter
removal, nutrient transformation, biomass growth, solids handling, and aeration control in one coupled process.
Mechanistic activated sludge models provide a scientific language for representing these interactions, but
their practical use can become difficult when many operating conditions or influent scenarios must be examined
\citep{Henze1999,Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. Scenario screening, operational assessment, and process exploration
therefore benefit from predictive tools that can approximate mechanistic behavior more quickly while retaining
enough fidelity to support engineering interpretation.

Machine learning surrogates are attractive for this purpose because they can learn nonlinear relationships
from structured process data and return predictions at much lower computational cost than repeated mechanistic
simulation. Wastewater treatment studies increasingly use data driven models for effluent prediction,
operational decision support, energy assessment, and plant-performance analysis
\citep{Newhart2019,Asadi2017,Mao2024,Sadeghassadi2018,Croll2023,Wang2025,Yang2025}. This growing literature
shows that machine learning can be useful in wastewater applications, but it does not by itself establish
which modern tabular learners are most effective as raw surrogates of an activated sludge simulator.

The gap is partly methodological and partly substantive. Many wastewater prediction studies emphasize plant
monitoring data, selected effluent indicators, or a small number of model families. These studies are valuable,
but they make it difficult to separate the learnability of the activated sludge response from site-specific
noise, limited target definitions, or algorithm-specific choices. A controlled surrogate benchmark is needed
to show whether a broad set of state of the art tabular models can learn the nonlinear response of an
activated sludge process from paired inputs and outputs before any post prediction correction or additional
physical enforcement is considered \citep{Borisov2024,ShwartzZiv2022}.

Such a baseline matters because raw predictive accuracy is the first requirement of a useful surrogate. If
the unadjusted model output cannot recover the behavior of the mechanistic simulator, then later use in
screening, comparison, or decision support will rest on a weak empirical foundation. If raw predictions are
accurate, however, they establish that contemporary tabular models can capture important nonlinear structure
in the activated sludge response. This provides a focused basis for comparing model families, data efficiency,
and robustness under changes in operating conditions without conflating raw learning ability with downstream
correction procedures.

\section{Method}

The analysis used a fixed dataset generated from the mechanistic activated sludge simulator under the protocol
described below. The same in-distribution samples, beyond-domain samples, train-validation splits, selected
model configurations, preprocessing rules, and raw surrogate predictions were used throughout the evaluation.
No additional simulator runs, fold reassignments, hyperparameter searches, or alternative preprocessing rules
were introduced after the dataset and model protocol were finalized.

\subsection{Data Source and Prediction Scope}

The data were generated from a standalone, steady-state continuous stirred tank reactor represented by the
Activated Sludge Model No.\ 2d with two-step nitrification (ASM2d-TSN). The process description retained the 20
activated sludge components and 28 biological and chemical processes associated with hydrolysis, heterotrophic
and phosphate-accumulating-organism metabolism, two-step nitrification and denitrification, lysis, and
precipitation-redissolution. The model basis followed the Activated Sludge Model family documentation of
\citet{Henze2006}, while
the two-step nitrification formulation, stoichiometry, and rate coefficients followed
\citet{Guerrero2011}. This mechanistic simulator supplied the reference input-output pairs used to train and
evaluate the statistical surrogates.

Only raw surrogate predictions were analyzed. Scoring used the unadjusted effluent component vectors returned
by the fitted surrogates. Predictions were not clipped, reconciled, projected, or otherwise corrected after
inference. This endpoint measures whether tabular surrogates can approximate the nonlinear activated sludge
mapping from the same inputs and outputs used by the mechanistic benchmark.

\subsection{Mechanistic Benchmark and Prediction Task}

The in-distribution design sampled hydraulic retention time, aeration, and 20 influent component
concentrations, giving 22 continuous input variables. A randomized, scrambled strength-one Latin hypercube was
generated with root seed 42 and linearly scaled to the prespecified operating and influent bounds
\citep{McKay1979}. Each sampled input was passed to the steady-state ASM2d-TSN simulator. The steady-state
equations were solved by nonlinear least squares under componentwise state bounds from a lower numerical floor
to an upper bound of 1500 in each component's native unit. Solver cost-change, step-size, and gradient
tolerances were set to \(10^{-9}\), and each nonlinear solve was limited to 1200 function evaluations. Two
physically informed initial guesses were attempted before a 20-day stiff backward differentiation formula
dynamic relaxation was used as a fallback initializer. Accepted states were required to converge with a
maximum absolute balance residual no greater than \(10^{-9}\). The in-distribution dataset retained 10,000
accepted steady states.

The retained steady states were converted into a supervised tabular regression problem. The input vector for
each row consisted of hydraulic retention time, aeration, and the complete influent component vector. The
target was the corresponding 20-component effluent state. Reported composite quantities such as chemical
oxygen demand, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and total suspended solids were computed after component
prediction using a fixed component-to-composite composition map. These composites were therefore
interpretive summaries of the predicted component state rather than separately fitted response variables.

Two beyond-domain validation sets were generated by the same simulator and acceptance rules. These sets
evaluated raw surrogate behavior outside the original sampling envelope without introducing post prediction
correction. Each beyond-domain regime contains 600 accepted steady states. The mild regime perturbed selected
inputs 10\% to 25\% beyond their original upper bounds, while the severe regime perturbed them 35\% to 60\%
beyond those bounds. The perturbed features were hydraulic retention time, aeration, influent ammonium
nitrogen, and influent slowly biodegradable substrate. Candidate generation used a mixture dominated by
one-feature excursions with a smaller share of two-feature excursions, while all other inputs remained inside
their in-distribution ranges.

\subsection{Surrogate Models and Preprocessing}

Thirteen statistical surrogates were evaluated from their raw predictions. The model set was chosen to
represent the main families commonly used for structured tabular regression, including boosted trees,
randomized tree ensembles, kernel and neighbor methods, latent-variable regression, sparse multi-task linear
regression, and neural-network surrogates. This selection allowed flexible nonlinear learners to be compared with simpler or
more inspectable alternatives while holding the simulation data, folds, and scoring rules fixed.
The model families draw on established surrogate and tabular-learning foundations, including random forests,
gradient boosting, partial least squares, and attentive tabular neural networks
\citep{Breiman2001,ChenGuestrin2016,Wold2001,ArikPfister2021,Borisov2024}.

The workflow did not use missing-value
imputation, response-dependent outlier removal, or target filtering. Consequently, the reported raw-prediction
results reflect each surrogate's learned mapping under a consistent and leakage-controlled preprocessing
scheme.

\subsection{Training, Tuning, and Validation Protocol}

In-distribution performance was evaluated with nested sample sizes of 500, 1450, 2400, 3350, 4300, 5250,
6200, 7150, 8100, 9050, and 10,000 rows. A seed-42 permutation established the nested row order and persistent
master fold assignments before any subset was formed. At every sample size, five-fold cross-validation used
the same validation folds for every surrogate. Each fold therefore trained on four fifths of the selected
subset and scored the remaining fifth. The common folds made the raw surrogate comparisons depend on model
behavior rather than on different validation samples.

Hyperparameters were selected without access to the scored outer fold. Within each largest-scale outer
training partition, a four-fold inner search minimized component-standardized mean squared error. The selected
setting for a given outer fold was then held fixed when the same surrogate was evaluated on smaller nested
subsets belonging to that fold. This design made the learning curves a controlled sample-size analysis rather
than a sequence of separately optimized small-data studies. Hyperparameter optimization used seeded Optuna
studies with a tree-structured Parzen estimator and no pruning \citep{Akiba2019}. Each surrogate had five
outer-fold studies at the largest in-distribution scale and one additional full in-distribution study for the
beyond-domain refit, with 100 trials per study.

After the in-distribution cross-validation analysis, one final setting per surrogate was selected using only
the 10,000 in-distribution states. Each surrogate and its required preprocessing were then refitted on the full
in-distribution pool before raw predictions were made for the mild and severe beyond-domain sets. No
beyond-domain response was used for refitting, scaling, calibration, or hyperparameter selection.

\subsection{Raw Prediction Evaluation}

The primary outcome was accuracy of the 20-component effluent prediction. Component errors were scored in
standardized form so that components expressed in different physical units could contribute to one aggregate
assessment. The raw-prediction metrics were normalized mean squared error, normalized root mean squared error
(nRMSE), normalized mean absolute error, and the macro-average coefficient of determination across effluent
components. The training reference set associated with the scored fold supplied the component standard deviations used for
normalization. Fold-level results were summarized descriptively as means and sample standard deviations across
the five outer folds. No hypothesis tests were applied.

Learning curves were constructed from the same fold summaries across the nested sample sizes. These curves
showed how quickly each surrogate converted additional mechanistic simulations into lower raw prediction
error, which is central to judging whether the activated sludge mapping was learnable from tabular
input-output data \citep{VieringLoog2023}. Component-specific physical-unit errors were retained for
diagnosing which effluent components were easier or harder to approximate. Composite chemical oxygen demand,
total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and total suspended solids metrics were treated as secondary summaries
computed from the raw component predictions.

Beyond-domain evaluation used the full-data refit of each surrogate and the two separately simulated
validation regimes. The same raw component and composite metrics were computed for the mild and severe
datasets, using the in-distribution training pool as the normalization reference. These beyond-domain scores
were interpreted descriptively over the 600 retained cases in each regime. They were not averaged across folds
because the beyond-domain predictions came from one final refit per surrogate. Elapsed-time measurements were
used only to characterize the computational cost of fitting, preprocessing, and raw inference.

A surrogate was judged successful to the extent that its unadjusted predictions recovered the simulated
effluent component state, improved with additional training data, and retained useful accuracy under
controlled beyond-domain shifts. The endpoint was predictive approximation of the mechanistic simulator's
component outputs, with water-quality composites used only as downstream summaries of those component
predictions.

\section{Synthesis}

The results support the central premise that modern tabular machine learning surrogates can learn a
multicomponent activated sludge simulator response from paired input-output data. The strongest evidence is
the performance of the MLP at the largest sample size. Its low nRMSE, high macro-average coefficient of
determination, strong composite metrics, and very low raw inference latency show that the simulator response
contains learnable nonlinear structure that can be captured by a flexible data driven model. This finding is
consistent with the broad role of neural networks as nonlinear approximators and with their use in chemical
and wastewater process modeling \citep{Hornik1989,Curteanu2011,Sadeghassadi2018}.

The comparison also shows why a single surrogate family should not be assumed to dominate under every data
condition. Boosted trees were the strongest low-data learners, with XGBoost and LightGBM leading at 500
training cases. Their advantage at small sample size is consistent with the wider tabular-learning literature,
where tree-based methods often remain strong baselines for structured datasets \citep{Borisov2024,ShwartzZiv2022}.
As more simulated data became available, the MLP surpassed the boosted trees and retained the best
beyond-domain accuracy. TabNet followed a related but less favorable trajectory, starting from poor small-data
performance and improving sharply with more data. The learning curves therefore show that sample size changes
the preferred surrogate family, which is why surrogate selection should be evaluated across more than one
training-set size \citep{VieringLoog2023}.

The weaker performance of PLS, the multi-task linear models, k-NN, Random Forest, and Extra Trees clarifies
the character of the activated sludge prediction problem. Linear and latent-variable structure alone was not
enough to recover the full component response with high accuracy, even though those models were fast and more
inspectable. Neighbor averaging and randomized tree averaging also underperformed relative to boosted trees
and neural networks. The response surface therefore appears to require flexible nonlinear representation, but
not all nonlinear learners benefited equally from the available data. The strongest raw surrogates were those
that combined nonlinear flexibility with effective use of the tabular training structure.

The component-level findings add an important qualification. Aggregate accuracy did not mean that all
effluent components were equally easy to predict. Several nitrogen-linked and substrate-related components
remained more difficult across surrogate families, while alkalinity and inert components were comparatively
easier. This pattern is chemically plausible because activated sludge nutrient and substrate transformations
are coupled through nonlinear microbial pathways rather than through independent one-component responses
\citep{Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. Consequently, model assessment should retain component-level diagnostics even
when composite water-quality metrics are strong.

The beyond-domain results show that raw surrogate learning remained useful outside the sampled operating
envelope but became less reliable as the shift intensified. The MLP and TabNet retained the best severe-regime
accuracy, whereas boosted trees experienced a larger increase in error and the weaker baselines degraded
substantially. This behavior is consistent with the general expectation that data driven models are less
secure when evaluated outside the support of their training data, even when they interpolate well within the
sampled domain \citep{Karniadakis2021,Shen2023,Valente2025}. The raw surrogates are therefore appropriate for
rapid screening within and near the simulated domain, but beyond-domain use should be interpreted as
extrapolative prediction rather than as guaranteed process fidelity.

Taken together, the results establish a raw-prediction baseline for activated sludge surrogates. Flexible
tabular models, especially the MLP and the leading boosted trees, can approximate the mechanistic component
response with useful accuracy and very low inference cost. The evidence also shows that raw accuracy depends
on model family, training-set size, component identity, and degree of domain shift. The most defensible
interpretation is therefore neither that any machine learning model is sufficient nor that mechanistic
simulation can be casually replaced. Rather, the findings show that carefully validated raw surrogates can
serve as fast approximations of activated sludge simulator behavior, provided that their component-level and
beyond-domain limitations remain visible in the evaluation.


\chapter{Mass Conservation and Nonnegativity Enforcement on Machine Learning Surrogates of the Activated Sludge Process}
\label{ch:conservation}

\section{Rationale}

Once a surrogate has demonstrated that the activated sludge response is learnable from paired simulation data,
a second credibility problem remains. The prediction is not only a numerical estimate of several effluent
quantities; it is a component vector that should still be interpretable as an activated sludge state. The
Activated Sludge Model family represents carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, alkalinity, and solids through coupled
stoichiometric relations, and those relations make conservation and nonnegativity part of the meaning of the
predicted state \citep{Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. A surrogate that is close to the mechanistic target under
ordinary error metrics can still return an output that violates those requirements, leaving a gap between
statistical accuracy and physical admissibility \citep{Hansen2024,KircherVotsmeier2025}.

This gap is important because complete component predictions are often the basis for downstream water-quality
interpretation. Composite quantities such as chemical oxygen demand, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and
total suspended solids summarize the component state, but they do not by themselves prove that the underlying
vector satisfies the process structure from which those summaries are derived. A component vector can therefore
look useful at the aggregate level while containing a negative concentration or violating a declared
conservation relation. In wastewater treatment analysis, such an output is difficult to treat as a credible
process state, particularly when predictions may be used for screening, comparison, optimization, or decision
support \citep{Araujo2013,BhosekarIerapetritou2018,Durkin2024,Mao2024}.

The scientific machine learning literature offers several ways to connect learned predictions with physical
structure. Physics informed training can place governing relations into the loss function, while hard
enforcement approaches can use analytic constraints, specialized architectures, conservative formulations, or
projection onto physically consistent sets \citep{Raissi2019,Beucler2021,Hansen2024,SturmWexler2020,SturmWexler2022,Valente2025}.
For activated sludge surrogates, the relevant requirement is joint enforcement. A conservation-only correction
can still leave negative concentrations, whereas a positivity-only transformation can disturb the
sample-specific balance relation. The post prediction projection of \citet{KircherVotsmeier2025} is therefore
especially relevant because it addresses conservation and nonnegativity together after the surrogate has
already produced its component prediction.

The remaining question is how that enforcement behaves in an activated sludge surrogate setting. A correction
that guarantees admissibility is valuable only if its empirical consequences are visible: it may improve error
when raw residuals point away from the feasible balance relation, but it may also redistribute error among
components or composites. The motivating problem is therefore no longer whether machine learning can
approximate the simulator at all, but whether outputs from accurate raw surrogates can be converted into
physically admissible component states while preserving transparent evidence about predictive error, data
scarcity, computational cost, and extrapolation. Paired raw and projected diagnostics are needed to determine
whether post prediction enforcement strengthens activated sludge surrogate predictions without treating
physical admissibility as a substitute for ordinary validation.

\section{Method}

The analysis evaluated predictive error and physical admissibility through paired raw and projected surrogate
outputs. A steady-state activated sludge benchmark supplied complete influent and effluent component vectors.
The stoichiometric structure of the mechanistic model was then used to define a sample-specific conservation
target and a nonnegative feasible set. Each statistical surrogate first produced an uncorrected 20-component
prediction, and the same post prediction projection was then applied to that prediction. This pairing made the
effect of projection visible because the raw and projected outputs used the same training rows, validation
rows, preprocessing rules, surrogate settings, and mechanistic reference targets.

\subsection{Simulated Process and Prediction Target}

The benchmark data were generated from a standalone, steady-state continuous stirred tank reactor represented
by the Activated Sludge Model No.\ 2d with two-step nitrification (ASM2d-TSN). The process model retained 20
activated sludge components and 28 biological and chemical processes associated with hydrolysis,
heterotrophic and phosphate-accumulating-organism metabolism, two-step nitrification and denitrification,
lysis, and precipitation-redissolution. The activated sludge model basis followed \citet{Henze2006}, while the
two-step nitrification formulation, stoichiometry, and rate coefficients followed \citet{Guerrero2011}.

Each simulated case used 22 continuous inputs: hydraulic retention time (HRT), aeration, and the 20 influent
component concentrations. The effluent target was the corresponding 20-component steady-state vector. The
dissolved components were dissolved oxygen \(S_O\), fermentable substrate \(S_F\), acetate \(S_A\), ammonium
nitrogen \(S_{NH4}\), nitrite nitrogen \(S_{NO2}\), nitrate nitrogen \(S_{NO3}\), dinitrogen \(S_{N2}\),
soluble phosphate \(S_{PO4}\), soluble inert organic matter \(S_I\), and alkalinity \(S_{ALK}\). The
particulate components were particulate inert organic matter \(X_I\), slowly biodegradable substrate \(X_S\),
heterotrophic biomass \(X_H\), phosphate-accumulating-organism biomass \(X_{PAO}\), stored polyphosphate
\(X_{PP}\), stored polyhydroxyalkanoates \(X_{PHA}\), ammonia-oxidizing biomass \(X_{AOB}\),
nitrite-oxidizing biomass \(X_{NOB}\), precipitated metal phosphate \(X_{MeP}\), and metal hydroxide
\(X_{MeOH}\).

Chemical oxygen demand (COD), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and total suspended solids (TSS)
were computed only after component prediction. For any component vector \(c\in\mathbb{R}^{F}\), with \(F=20\),
the reported composite vector was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_composite_map}
y=(\mathrm{COD},\mathrm{TN},\mathrm{TP},\mathrm{TSS})^\top=I_{\mathrm{comp}}c,
\end{equation}
where \(I_{\mathrm{comp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times F}\) is the fixed component-to-composite composition matrix.
The same map was applied to raw and projected component predictions, so the surrogates were not trained
directly on the four composites.

The in-distribution design used a randomized, scrambled strength-one Latin hypercube with seed 42
\citep{McKay1979}. The sampled variables and solver rules are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:ch2_simulation_protocol}. Each sampled input was solved as a steady-state mechanistic case
using nonlinear least squares under componentwise state bounds from \(10^{-8}\) to 1500 in the native unit of
each component. Solver cost-change, step-size, and gradient tolerances were \(10^{-9}\), and each nonlinear
solve was limited to 1200 function evaluations. Two physically informed steady-state initializations were
attempted first; if both failed, a 20-day stiff backward differentiation formula dynamic relaxation supplied a
fallback initializer. Accepted states were required to have a maximum absolute steady-state balance residual no
greater than \(10^{-9}\). The in-distribution dataset retained 10,000 accepted steady states.

\subsection{Conservation Invariants and Feasible Component Space}

Let \(c_{in,i},c_{out,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}\) denote the influent and accepted mechanistic effluent component
vectors for sample \(i\), and let \(\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times F}\) be the ASM2d-TSN Petersen matrix with
\(P=28\) processes and \(F=20\) components. The numerical rank of \(\nu\) was 15, giving a five-dimensional
right null space. If the columns of \(V_0\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times K}\), with \(K=5\), form an orthonormal basis
for this right null space, then the invariant operator was defined as \(A=V_0^\top\). Thus,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_invariant_operator}
A\nu^\top=V_0^\top\nu^\top=(\nu V_0)^\top=\mathbf{0}_{K\times P},
\qquad
AA^\top=V_0^\top V_0=I_K.
\end{equation}
Each row of \(A\) is therefore a linearly independent invariant of the biological and chemical reaction
network.

For the adopted steady-state system boundary, the component balance for sample \(i\) can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_steady_balance}
D_i(c_{out,i}-c_{in,i})=\nu^\top\rho_i+\omega_i e_{S_O},
\end{equation}
where \(D_i>0\) is the hydraulic dilution rate, \(\rho_i\in\mathbb{R}^{P}\) is the vector of biological and
chemical process rates evaluated at \(c_{out,i}\), and \(\omega_i e_{S_O}\) is the external dissolved-oxygen
transfer term. Premultiplication by \(A\) eliminates the reaction contribution because \(A\nu^\top=\mathbf{0}\).
The dissolved-oxygen transfer vector is also in the range of \(\nu^\top\), so \(Ae_{S_O}=\mathbf{0}\) to
numerical precision. The balance therefore reduces to the sample-specific invariant relation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_invariant_balance}
Ac_{out,i}=Ac_{in,i}.
\end{equation}

The conservation target and feasible effluent set for sample \(i\) were defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_feasible_set}
b_i:=Ac_{in,i},\qquad
\mathcal{F}_i=\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{F}:Ac=b_i,\ c\geq\mathbf{0}\}.
\end{equation}
The equality in Equation~\eqref{eq:ch2_feasible_set} defines the five stoichiometric conservation invariants
for the stated steady-state boundary, while \(c\geq\mathbf{0}\) imposes componentwise nonnegativity. The
sampled influent vectors were nonnegative, so each \(c_{in,i}\) provided a feasible anchor for projection. In
this analysis, mass conservation means satisfaction of these five stoichiometric invariants; it does not claim
every possible elemental, kinetic, or plant-wide balance.

\subsection{Post Prediction Projection}

The positivity-preserving conservative projection of \citet{KircherVotsmeier2025} was applied after surrogate
prediction and did not alter the fitted surrogate. With \(u_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\) containing HRT and aeration,
the complete input vector was \(x_i=[u_i^\top,c_{in,i}^\top]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{22}\). A trained surrogate
\(f_m:\mathbb{R}^{22}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{F}\) first produced the raw component prediction
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_raw_prediction}
c_{raw,i}^{(m)}=f_m(x_i).
\end{equation}
The projection then formed a positive provisional target and a relative scaling matrix,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_positive_scaling}
c_{+,i}^{(m)}=\max\!\left(c_{raw,i}^{(m)},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_c\right),\qquad
s_i^{(m)}=\mathbf{1}\oslash c_{+,i}^{(m)},\qquad
W_{s,i}^{(m)}=\operatorname{Diag}\!\left(s_i^{(m)}\right),
\end{equation}
where the maximum and division are componentwise and each entry of \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_c\) is
\(10^{-8}\) in the native unit of its component. The lower-bounded vector \(c_{+,i}^{(m)}\) supplied the
projection target and its weights; it was not reported as a clipped prediction.

Let \(Z\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times(F-K)}\) have orthonormal columns spanning \(\operatorname{null}(A)\). Because
\(Ac_{in,i}=b_i\), every vector satisfying the conservation equality can be represented as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_conservative_parameterization}
\{c:Ac=b_i\}=\{c_{in,i}+Zq:q\in\mathbb{R}^{F-K}\}.
\end{equation}
The projection selected the conservative change \(Zq\) closest to the provisional raw-prediction change under
the relative scaling in Equation~\eqref{eq:ch2_positive_scaling}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_scaled_projection}
q_i^{*,(m)}=\argmin_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{F-K}}
\left\|W_{s,i}^{(m)}\left[Zq-\left(c_{+,i}^{(m)}-c_{in,i}\right)\right]\right\|_2.
\end{equation}
The resulting conservative candidate was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_conservative_candidate}
c_{cand,i}^{(m)}=c_{in,i}+Zq_i^{*,(m)}.
\end{equation}
This candidate satisfies \(Ac_{cand,i}^{(m)}=b_i\). The weighted least-squares system was solved by an
orthogonal-triangular factorization, with singular-value decomposition used as a numerical fallback and no
normal-equation inverse formed.

The candidate was then shortened toward the feasible influent anchor only when needed to ensure
nonnegativity. Let \(\delta_i^{(m)}=c_{cand,i}^{(m)}-c_{in,i}\). The backtracking factor and final projected
prediction were
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_backtracking}
\alpha_i^{(m)}=
\begin{cases}
1, & \min_j c_{cand,ij}^{(m)}\geq0,\\
(1-\eta)\displaystyle\min_{j:\delta_{ij}^{(m)}<0}
\frac{c_{in,ij}}{-\delta_{ij}^{(m)}}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\tilde c_i^{(m)}=c_{in,i}+\alpha_i^{(m)}\delta_i^{(m)},
\end{equation}
with \(\eta=10^{-12}\). The construction preserves conservation because
\(A\tilde c_i^{(m)}=Ac_{in,i}+\alpha_i^{(m)}AZq_i^{*,(m)}=b_i\), and it enforces componentwise
nonnegativity by restricting the movement along any direction that would cross the nonnegative boundary. A
projection was accepted when \(\|A\tilde c_i^{(m)}-b_i\|_2\leq10^{-10}\) and every component satisfied
\(\tilde c_{ij}^{(m)}\geq-10^{-10}\) in its native unit. Backtracking activation and projection latency were
retained as diagnostics.

All transformations were fitted only on the relevant inner-training, outer-training, or full in-distribution
training partition. The fitted transformations were then applied unchanged to the matching validation or
beyond-domain inputs. When a surrogate was trained on target z-scores, its predictions were inverse-transformed
to physical component units before projection, conservation evaluation, nonnegativity evaluation, and composite
calculation. No missing-value imputation, response-dependent outlier removal, or target filtering was used.

Hyperparameters were selected without access to the scored outer fold. At the largest in-distribution scale,
each outer training partition used a four-fold inner search to minimize component-standardized mean squared
error. The selected setting for that outer fold was then held fixed when the same surrogate was refitted on the
smaller nested training subsets. This made the learning curves a controlled sample-size analysis rather than a
sequence of separately optimized small-data studies. Hyperparameter optimization used seeded Optuna studies
with a tree-structured Parzen estimator, no pruning, and 100 trials per study \citep{Akiba2019}. A separate
full in-distribution search selected the setting used for the final refit before beyond-domain evaluation.

\subsection{Cross-Validation and Paired Projection Workflow}

In-distribution evaluation used nested training-set sizes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_sample_grid}
N\in\{500,1450,2400,3350,4300,5250,6200,7150,8100,9050,10000\}.
\end{equation}
A seed-42 permutation established both the nested row order and persistent fold assignments before any subset
was formed. At every sample size, five-fold cross-validation used the same validation folds for every
surrogate. Each fold trained on four fifths of the selected subset and scored the remaining fifth. The common
folds made raw-versus-projected comparisons paired by sample, model, and training context.

\subsection{Accuracy, Constraint, and Timing Metrics}

Component-space accuracy was primary because the 20 effluent components were the supervised targets and because
conservation and nonnegativity are defined before aggregation. Let \(g\in\{1,\ldots,5\}\) index the outer fold
at dataset size \(N\), and let \(\mathcal{T}_{N,g}\) be its training partition. For prediction type
\(t\in\{\mathrm{raw},\mathrm{proj}\}\), define \(c_i^{(m,\mathrm{raw})}=c_{raw,i}^{(m)}\) and
\(c_i^{(m,\mathrm{proj})}=\tilde c_i^{(m)}\). The standardized component residual for validation sample \(i\)
and component \(j\) was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_standardized_residual}
\xi_{ij}^{(m,t,N,g)}
=\frac{c_{ij}^{(m,t)}-c_{out,ij}}
{\sigma_j^{(\mathcal{T}_{N,g})}},
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_j^{(\mathcal{T}_{N,g})}\) is the population standard deviation of component \(j\) in the
applicable training partition. For the \(n=N/5\) observations in the scored fold, the normalized mean squared
error (nMSE), normalized root mean squared error (nRMSE), and normalized mean absolute error (nMAE) were
\begin{align}
\mathrm{nMSE}_{m,t,N,g}
&=\frac{1}{Fn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{F}
\left(\xi_{ij}^{(m,t,N,g)}\right)^2, \nonumber\\
\mathrm{nRMSE}_{m,t,N,g}
&=\sqrt{\mathrm{nMSE}_{m,t,N,g}}, \label{eq:ch2_normalized_metrics}\\
\mathrm{nMAE}_{m,t,N,g}
&=\frac{1}{Fn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{F}
\left|\xi_{ij}^{(m,t,N,g)}\right|. \nonumber
\end{align}
Component \(R^2\) values and their equal-weight macro-average were computed from residual and total
sum-of-squares terms:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_coefficient_determination}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{SSE}_{j,m,t,N,g}
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(c_{ij}^{(m,t)}-c_{out,ij}\right)^2,\\
\mathrm{SST}_{j,N,g}
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(c_{out,ij}-\bar c_{out,j,N,g}\right)^2,\\
R_{j,m,t,N,g}^{2}
&=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}_{j,m,t,N,g}}{\mathrm{SST}_{j,N,g}},\\
\bar R_{m,t,N,g}^{2}
&=\frac{1}{F}\sum_{j=1}^{F}R_{j,m,t,N,g}^{2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Component-specific errors in physical units and composite errors for COD, TN, TP, and TSS were retained as
secondary diagnostics. They were not mixed into the primary 20-component standardized score.

Mass-conservation and nonnegativity compliance were assessed before component aggregation. With the unrounded
orthonormal basis \(A\), the conservation residual and standardized negative magnitude were
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_physical_residuals}
v_{mc,i}(c)=\left\|Ac-b_i\right\|_2,\qquad
v_{nn,i}^{\mathrm{std}}\!\left(c;\mathcal{T}_{N,g}\right)
=\left\|\min\!\left(c\oslash\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(\mathcal{T}_{N,g})},
\mathbf{0}\right)\right\|_1.
\end{equation}
The minimum is componentwise. Conservation and nonnegativity violation frequencies were
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_violation_frequencies}
\begin{aligned}
\pi_{mc}^{(m,t,N,g)}
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathbb{1}\!\left[v_{mc,i}\!\left(c_i^{(m,t)}\right)>10^{-10}\right],\\
\pi_{nn}^{(m,t,N,g)}
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathbb{1}\!\left[\exists j:\ c_{ij}^{(m,t)}<-10^{-10}\right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Mean, median, and maximum residual magnitudes were retained alongside these frequencies because rare large
violations can be hidden by average residuals.

The paired effect of projection on the primary accuracy score and the standardized component displacement were
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch2_projection_effects}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\mathrm{nRMSE}_{m,N,g}
&=\mathrm{nRMSE}_{m,\mathrm{proj},N,g}
-\mathrm{nRMSE}_{m,\mathrm{raw},N,g},\\
d_{c,i}^{\mathrm{std},(m)}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_{N,g}\right)
&=\left\|\left(\tilde c_i^{(m)}-c_{raw,i}^{(m)}\right)
\oslash\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(\mathcal{T}_{N,g})}\right\|_2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Negative \(\Delta\mathrm{nRMSE}\) denotes an accuracy benefit from projection, while positive values denote an
accuracy cost. Fold summaries used the five paired fold differences rather than subtracting separately averaged
raw and projected scores.

Wall-clock timing was partitioned into preprocessing and surrogate fitting, raw batch inference, projection
alone, and end-to-end projected inference. Hyperparameter-search duration was reported separately from fitting
and inference. In-distribution timing used a deterministic 512-row validation batch, five warm-up calls, and 30
timed repetitions. The median 512-row latency within a fold was divided by 512 and reported in milliseconds
per sample. Projection timing included scaling, the weighted solve, conservation and nonnegativity checks, and
positivity backtracking.

\subsection{Beyond-Domain Evaluation}

After cross-validation, one final setting per surrogate was selected using only the 10,000 in-distribution
states. Each surrogate and its required preprocessing were then refitted on the complete in-distribution pool
before any beyond-domain response was used for scoring. The beyond-domain validation sets were generated with
new mechanistic simulations and the same solver, initialization sequence, and acceptance rule used for the
in-distribution data. No beyond-domain response was used for refitting, scaling, calibration, or hyperparameter
selection.

Four input features were perturbed beyond their in-distribution upper bounds: HRT, aeration, influent ammonium
nitrogen, and influent slowly biodegradable substrate. For a perturbed feature \(\ell\) with in-distribution
bounds \([L_\ell,U_\ell]\), the normalized upper-bound exceedance was
\((x_{i\ell}-U_\ell)/(U_\ell-L_\ell)\). Mild excursions placed this quantity between 0.10 and 0.25, while
severe excursions placed it between 0.35 and 0.60. Candidate generation was dominated by one-feature
excursions, with a smaller share of two-feature excursions, and all unperturbed inputs remained inside their
in-distribution ranges. Each retained beyond-domain point was therefore outside the in-distribution
hyperrectangle.

results were descriptive over 600 cases from one full-data refit per surrogate rather than five-fold
cross-validation estimates. Predictive extrapolation and physical admissibility were interpreted separately.

\subsection{Reproducibility Controls}

All pseudorandom streams were derived from root seed 42. The same row order, fold assignments, validation
samples, and beyond-domain regimes were used across surrogate families. Seeded fitting was used for XGBoost,
LightGBM, CatBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest, Extra Trees, MLP, and TabNet; SVR, k-NN, PLS, and the multi-task
linear models were deterministic for fixed data and selected parameters. Double precision was used for
mechanistic states, invariant construction, preprocessing, returned predictions, projection, and metrics,
except for the internal tensors of TabNet. These controls were intended to make differences between raw and
projected outputs attributable to the post prediction projection rather than to changing folds, preprocessing,
or random sampling.

\section{Results}

The results are organized around the paired question that motivated the analysis. First, the mechanistic
reference states were checked to verify that the benchmark itself occupied the intended physical domain. The
surrogate predictions were then evaluated in two forms: raw outputs returned directly by the trained learners
and projected outputs obtained after enforcing the declared conservation and nonnegativity conditions.

\subsection{Benchmark Verification}

The completed benchmark contained 10,000 in-distribution steady states and 600 accepted states in each
beyond-domain regime. Every mechanistic candidate in these three sets met the acceptance rule
(Table~\ref{tab:ch2_dataset_validation}). The accepted effluent states were componentwise positive, and their
invariant residuals were small relative to the steady-state acceptance tolerance of \(10^{-9}\). The largest
observed invariant residual was \(4.90\times10^{-11}\) in-distribution, \(5.67\times10^{-11}\) in the mild
beyond-domain regime, and \(2.91\times10^{-10}\) in the severe beyond-domain regime.

These checks show that violations observed in raw surrogate outputs were not inherited from infeasible
mechanistic targets. The reference states already satisfied the intended component domain before any surrogate
was trained. Projection was therefore tested as a post prediction correction of learned outputs, not as a repair
of the mechanistic data.

\subsection{Mass Conservation and Nonnegativity}

Every raw surrogate prediction set violated the declared mass-conservation tolerance at every sample size. This
included models whose raw predictions remained nonnegative at \(N=10,000\), such as AdaBoost, Random Forest,
Extra Trees, and \(k\)-NN (Table~\ref{tab:ch2_projection_physics}). Their nonnegative raw outputs were still
not guaranteed to satisfy the case-specific conservation relation \(Ac=b_i\). Conversely, the more flexible
unconstrained regressors often violated both requirements: at full size, raw nonnegativity violation rates
ranged from 39.0\% for LightGBM to 76.8\% for support vector regression (SVR) among the surrogates that crossed
the zero boundary.

Projection eliminated both violation types in all 750,750 in-distribution projected predictions. The largest
observed projected conservation residual was \(2.06\times10^{-13}\), and no projected component was negative
under the stated tolerance. This is the central physical-admissibility result: the same post prediction
procedure returned every evaluated surrogate output to the declared invariant balance and the nonnegative
component domain without retraining the underlying models.

but when a raw prediction is far from the mechanistic target, enforcing feasibility can also move selected
components farther from their true values.

Every raw beyond-domain prediction set violated mass conservation. Projection reduced both mass-conservation
and nonnegativity violation rates to zero across all 15,600 beyond-domain projected predictions. The maximum
projected conservation residual was \(1.72\times10^{-13}\), and backtracking was activated for 68 predictions
(0.436\%). Across in-distribution and beyond-domain evaluation combined, all 766,350 projected predictions
satisfied the enforced physical requirements.

\subsection{Computational Cost}

Projection added a small per-sample latency relative to the cost of the complete surrogate workflow
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch2_inference_latency}). At \(N=10,000\), projection-only latency ranged from about 0.068 to
0.144 ms per sample across the surrogate set. End-to-end projected inference remained below 0.315 ms per sample
for all models except AdaBoost and SVR. The MLP combined the lowest in-distribution and beyond-domain errors
with an end-to-end projected inference latency of 0.107 ms per sample. These timings indicate that enforcing
the two physical requirements did not remove the computational advantage of surrogate prediction.

\section{Synthesis}

The results show that physical admissibility is a separate requirement from predictive accuracy. The raw
surrogates could learn the activated sludge simulator response with useful accuracy, especially when flexible
models were trained on the full in-distribution dataset. Yet every raw prediction set violated the declared
mass-conservation condition, and several accurate surrogates also produced negative values in low-concentration
components. This decoupling is the main empirical reason for treating ordinary error metrics and physical
diagnostics as complementary rather than interchangeable. A low nRMSE indicated closeness to the mechanistic
target, but it did not prove that the returned vector could be interpreted as an admissible activated sludge
state.

The projection addressed that specific admissibility gap. Across 766,350 projected predictions, it enforced
both conservation and nonnegativity with residuals at numerical precision and without changing the underlying
surrogate architecture, training loss, or hyperparameter protocol. This matters because component-space
predictions are not merely intermediate numerical outputs; they are the basis from which COD, TN, TP, and TSS
summaries are derived. If the component state violates a declared balance or contains a negative concentration,
then an apparently plausible composite value can hide a physically inconsistent prediction. The paired
diagnostics make that problem visible and show that a model-agnostic post prediction step can prevent such
outputs from entering downstream interpretation.

The same results also caution against treating projection as a device for improving accuracy. Projection
usually improved component-level scores, but aggregate nRMSE did not improve uniformly. Its effect depended on
the position of the raw residual relative to the feasible affine space, the relative weighting in the
correction, and the activation of positivity backtracking. The multi-task linear models benefited consistently
because projection removed a repeatable inconsistency among components. Random Forest and several averaging
models were already nonnegative but farther from the feasible balance relation; correcting them sometimes
required larger movements that increased aggregate error. The useful conclusion is not that projection makes
every surrogate better. It is that projection makes the physical consequences of each surrogate explicit.

Beyond-domain evaluation reinforces this distinction. Severe shifts increased error for every surrogate,
confirming that physical enforcement does not solve extrapolation by itself. Nevertheless, projection often
helped under beyond-domain conditions, especially for surrogates whose extrapolation errors contained larger
infeasible components. This behavior is consistent with the broader scientific machine learning view that
physical structure can constrain learned predictions while still leaving empirical generalization as a
separate problem \citep{Hansen2024,Shen2023,Valente2025}. For activated sludge surrogates, the practical
lesson is that admissibility checks should travel with accuracy checks, particularly when predictions are used
outside the center of the sampled operating domain.

Taken together, the evidence supports post prediction projection as a transparent enforcement layer for
activated sludge surrogate workflows when complete component states are required. Its strongest contribution is
not universal error reduction, but reliable removal of two physically meaningful failure modes: broken
stoichiometric invariants and negative concentrations. The limits are equally important. The guarantees apply
to the stated steady-state component basis, invariant operator, and nonnegativity condition; they do not prove
field validity, kinetic realism, or plant-wide conservation beyond the modeled boundary. Within that scope, the
workflow strengthens surrogate credibility by requiring a prediction to be both numerically evaluated and
physically interpretable before it is treated as a usable wastewater treatment state.


\chapter{A Physically Admissible and Interpretable Machine Learning Surrogate of the Activated Sludge Process}
\label{ch:admissible}

\section{Rationale}

Activated sludge surrogate modeling becomes most useful when the predicted output remains meaningful as a
complete process state, not only as a set of accurate numerical estimates. The Activated Sludge Model (ASM)
family represents wastewater treatment through coupled component balances for organic matter, nutrients,
alkalinity, biomass, and solids, so a predicted effluent vector should remain compatible with the component
space in which those balances are defined \citep{Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. Conventional machine learning
surrogates can approximate nonlinear simulator responses with low error, but low error alone does not ensure
mass conservation, nonnegativity, or an interpretable relationship between operating conditions, influent
composition, and effluent state \citep{Hansen2024,KircherVotsmeier2025,Shen2023}. The central motivation is
therefore to move from fast prediction toward a surrogate whose returned state is also physically admissible
and inspectable.

Two partial solutions leave this need unresolved when they are considered separately. A raw-prediction
benchmark can establish whether modern tabular learners recover the activated sludge response from paired
inputs and outputs, but the resulting predictions may still be unconstrained component vectors. A post
prediction enforcement layer can then return those vectors to the feasible set, but physical admissibility is
introduced as a correction after the surrogate has already produced its estimate. That workflow is valuable
for diagnosis and repair, yet it does not by itself create a surrogate whose learned structure is organized
around the physical roles of influent loads, operating variables, nonlinear effects, and component coupling.
For engineering use, the stronger target is an integrated surrogate design in which predictive accuracy,
physical admissibility, and interpretability are treated as connected requirements rather than as separate
afterthoughts.

Scientific machine learning provides the broader reason for this integration. Loss functions that encode
conservation, analytic constraints, conservative architectures, and projection based corrections show that
physical structure can be imposed on learned models, but soft penalties do not necessarily eliminate deployment
violations and corrections limited to equality constraints do not necessarily prevent negative states
\citep{Raissi2019,Beucler2021,Hansen2024,SturmWexler2020,SturmWexler2022,KircherVotsmeier2025,Valente2025}.
Activated sludge surrogates require these ideas to be adapted to a component-space prediction problem in which
the same effluent vector must support material-balance interpretation, nonnegative concentrations, and derived
water quality summaries. The motivating gap is not only the absence of a correction mechanism, but the absence
of a transparent surrogate formulation that joins learned nonlinear response with guaranteed final-state
admissibility under the declared ASM boundary.

Interpretability makes the gap sharper. In environmental engineering applications, a surrogate may be used for
screening operating conditions, comparing scenarios, or supporting optimization, so the model should provide
more than a black-box mapping from inputs to outputs when a transparent alternative is feasible. The argument
for interpretable models in high-consequence settings is that users should be able to inspect the basis of a
prediction rather than rely entirely on post hoc explanation \citep{Rudin2019}. In the activated sludge
setting, this means retaining visible roles for operating conditions, influent component loads, nonlinear
curvature, and cross-component redistribution while still enforcing the physical requirements that make the
returned vector credible as a process state.

Invariant-Constrained Second-Order Regression (ICSOR) is motivated by this combined requirement. Its role is
not simply to replace the most accurate unconstrained learner, nor simply to attach a correction step to an
otherwise opaque regression head. The research question is whether a structured, invariant-aware surrogate can
provide a practical balance among predictive accuracy, data efficiency, computational cost, interpretability,
and exact deployment-time admissibility. Such a balance is important because an activated sludge surrogate that
is slightly less flexible but physically dependable and structurally inspectable may be more useful for
engineering analysis than a lower-error model whose component predictions require separate repair and remain
difficult to interpret.

\section{Method}

The analysis used a steady-state activated sludge benchmark to evaluate whether Invariant-Constrained
Second-Order Regression could return predictions that were accurate, physically admissible, and structurally
inspectable. All models received the same operating and influent inputs and were trained against the same
20-component effluent state. The methodological distinction was that the comparator regressors learned a
direct unconstrained input-output map, whereas ICSOR learned a structured component-space regression head and
then returned predictions through a checked deployment rule.

\subsection{Simulated Process and Prediction Target}

The benchmark represented a standalone continuous stirred tank reactor governed by Activated Sludge Model
No.\ 2d with two-step nitrification (ASM2d-TSN). The process description retained 20 activated sludge
components and 28 biological and chemical processes, including hydrolysis, heterotrophic growth and
denitrification, phosphate-accumulating-organism storage and growth, two-step nitrification, lysis, and
phosphorus precipitation-redissolution. The component basis followed the Activated Sludge Model family
\citep{Henze2006}, and the two-step nitrification structure followed \citet{Guerrero2011}.

Each sample contained two operating variables and the full influent component state. The operating vector
\(u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\) contained hydraulic retention time (HRT) and aeration level. The influent vector
\(c_{in}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}\), with \(F=20\), contained dissolved oxygen, soluble carbon, nitrogen,
phosphorus, inert, and alkalinity states together with particulate inert, biodegradable substrate, biomass,
storage-polymer, and metal-precipitate states. The sampled symbols and ranges are given in
Table~\ref{tab:ch3_simulation_domain}. The supervised target was the corresponding effluent component vector
\(c_{out}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}\).

The 22-dimensional input domain in Table~\ref{tab:ch3_simulation_domain} was sampled by a randomized Latin
hypercube with seed 42 \citep{McKay1979}. Each sampled operating-influent point was solved as a steady-state
ASM2d-TSN case by nonlinear least squares under componentwise bounds from \(10^{-8}\) to 1500 in the native
unit of each component. The cost-change, step-size, and gradient tolerances were \(10^{-9}\), and each
nonlinear solve was limited to 1200 function evaluations. Physically informed initial guesses were attempted
before a dynamic-relaxation fallback was used. A candidate state was retained only when the maximum absolute
steady-state balance residual was no greater than \(10^{-9}\). The final benchmark contained 10,000 accepted
steady states.

Chemical oxygen demand (COD), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and total suspended solids (TSS)
were computed after component prediction rather than used as direct training targets. For any component vector
\(c\), the reported composite vector was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_composite_map}
y_{\mathrm{comp}}=I_{\mathrm{comp}}c,
\end{equation}
where \(I_{\mathrm{comp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times F}\) is the fixed component-to-composite composition matrix.
Keeping this map outside the learned model ensured that all methods were compared first in the activated
sludge component space where conservation and nonnegativity are defined.

\subsection{Stoichiometric Invariants and Feasible States}

Let \(\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{28\times F}\) denote the ASM2d-TSN stoichiometric matrix. A full-row-rank invariant
operator \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times F}\) was formed from the right null space of \(\nu\), so that
\(A\nu^\top=0\). The adopted stoichiometric matrix produced \(q=5\) independent invariant rows. For an
admissible steady state within the declared reactor boundary, the component change from influent to effluent
can be expressed through reaction progress variables \(\xi\), giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_stoichiometric_change}
c_{out}-c_{in}=\nu^\top\xi .
\end{equation}
Premultiplication by \(A\) removes the reaction term and gives the conservation relation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_invariant_relation}
A(c_{out}-c_{in})=0,\qquad Ac_{out}=Ac_{in}.
\end{equation}
This null-space construction follows the same conservation logic used in physically structured surrogate
models, where learned predictions are required to remain on a constraint manifold rather than only near the
observed target values \citep{SturmWexler2020,SturmWexler2022}.

For sample \(i\), the conservation target and feasible effluent set were
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_feasible_set}
b_i=Ac_{in,i},\qquad
\mathcal{F}_i=\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{F}:Ac=b_i,\ c\geq\mathbf{0}\}.
\end{equation}
The equality condition encodes the five modeled stoichiometric invariants, and the inequality condition
requires componentwise nonnegativity. These constraints define physical admissibility for the returned
component vector. They do not claim complete kinetic realism, thermodynamic feasibility, or plant-wide
conservation outside the adopted system boundary.

\subsection{Invariant-Constrained Second-Order Regression}

ICSOR separates the operating variables from the influent inventory when constructing its regression head.
For each sample, the feature vector was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_feature_map}
\phi(u,c_{in})=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
u\\
c_{in}\\
u\otimes u\\
c_{in}\otimes c_{in}\\
u\otimes c_{in}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{D},
\qquad
D=1+M_{op}+F+M_{op}^{2}+F^{2}+M_{op}F,
\end{equation}
with \(M_{op}=2\). This partition keeps the engineering roles of operating settings and influent loads
explicit. Operating variables alter the reactor environment, whereas influent concentrations define the
material inventory supplied to that environment \citep{Araujo2013,Machado2014}.

The feature vector drives a latent component-space forcing vector rather than the final deployed prediction
directly:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_latent_driver}
r(u,c_{in})=B\phi(u,c_{in}).
\end{equation}
Written by blocks, this driver is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_block_driver}
r=b+W_u u+W_{in}c_{in}
\,+\,\Theta_{uu}(u\otimes u)
\,+\,\Theta_{cc}(c_{in}\otimes c_{in})
\,+\,\Theta_{uc}(u\otimes c_{in}).
\end{equation}
The blocks respectively represent baseline forcing, first-order operating effects, first-order influent-load
effects, operating curvature, influent-state curvature, and operation-loading interactions. After each update
of \(B\), the self-quadratic blocks were symmetrized so that duplicated ordered products such as \(x_i x_j\)
and \(x_j x_i\) had the same fitted contribution. This step preserves fitted values while making the
quadratic coefficient blocks easier to inspect.

Cross-component dependence was represented through a learned coupling matrix
\(\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}\). With \(R=I_F-\Gamma\), the coupled regression relation was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_coupled_relation}
Rc=r(u,c_{in}).
\end{equation}
The diagonal of \(\Gamma\) was fixed to zero, its off-diagonal entries were bounded, and candidate updates
were accepted only when \(R\) remained well conditioned. Thus, the regression head could represent
redistribution among activated sludge components without allowing deployment to depend on a nearly singular
coupling matrix.

For a training set with feature matrix \(\Phi\), influent matrix \(C_{in}\), and effluent target matrix
\(C_{out}\), ICSOR estimated \(B\), \(\Gamma\), and an auxiliary nonnegative fitted-state matrix
\(\widehat{C}\) by minimizing
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_training_objective}
\begin{aligned}
(\widehat{B},\widehat{\Gamma},\widehat{C})
=\argmin_{B,\,\Gamma\in\mathcal{G},\,\widehat{C}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N\times F}}
&\ \left\|C_{out}-\widehat{C}\right\|_{F}^{2}
+\lambda_{inv}\left\|(\widehat{C}-C_{in})A^\top\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+\lambda_{sys}\left\|\widehat{C}(I_F-\Gamma)^\top-\Phi B^\top\right\|_{F}^{2}
+\lambda_B\|B\|_{F}^{2}
+\lambda_{\Gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{F}^{2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first term fits the effluent components directly. The invariant term is a soft training penalty rather
than a hard guarantee. The coupled-system term links the fitted states to the structured regression head, and
the ridge penalties stabilize the driver and coupling estimates. Exact conservation and nonnegativity are
therefore properties of the deployment rule described below, not of the soft training objective alone.

The objective was minimized through deterministic block-coordinate updates. For fixed \(\Gamma\) and
\(\widehat{C}\), the \(B\) update was a ridge-regularized least-squares problem. For fixed \(B\) and
\(\widehat{C}\), the \(\Gamma\) update was a convex box-constrained quadratic program within the admissible
coupling set \(\mathcal{G}\). For fixed \(B\) and \(\Gamma\), the \(\widehat{C}\) update was a nonnegative
quadratic program. Each full cycle updated the three blocks and retained the best objective value reached
under the selected stopping rules.

\subsection{Checked Deployment}

For a new input pair \((u_*,c_{in,*})\), the fitted driver and coupling matrix first define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_deployment_definitions}
\phi_*=\phi(u_*,c_{in,*}),\qquad
d_*=\widehat{B}\phi_*,\qquad
\widehat{R}=I_F-\widehat{\Gamma}.
\end{equation}
When \(\widehat{R}\) is nonsingular, the raw coupled state is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_raw_coupled_state}
c_{raw}=\widehat{R}^{-1}d_*.
\end{equation}
This state is the transparent regression-head prediction, but it is not automatically physically admissible.
The deployment rule therefore checks the raw state and, when needed, applies a staged correction.

The first correction stage is the nearest invariant-consistent affine projection:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_affine_projection}
c_{aff}
=\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{F}}\|c-c_{raw}\|_2^2
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Ac=Ac_{in,*}.
\end{equation}
Because \(A\) has full row rank, the closed-form solution is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_affine_closed_form}
c_{aff}=c_{raw}-A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}A(c_{raw}-c_{in,*}).
\end{equation}
This stage restores the invariant relation but does not by itself guarantee nonnegative component
concentrations.

The final branch is activated only when the raw or affine state fails the feasibility checks. It solves
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_deployment_lp}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{c}
=\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{F},\,\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{F}}
&\quad w_c^\top\rho\\
\text{subject to}\quad
& Ac=Ac_{in,*},\\
& c\geq\mathbf{0},\\
& -\rho\leq c-c_{aff}\leq\rho,\\
& \rho\geq\mathbf{0},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(w_c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}\) is a fixed vector of componentwise correction weights. The program keeps
the final state close to the affine reference in a weighted \(L_1\) sense while imposing both conservation and
nonnegativity. The feasible set is nonempty because the nonnegative influent state itself satisfies
\(Ac=Ac_{in,*}\) when \(c=c_{in,*}\). This checked deployment logic follows the same practical lesson emphasized by
\citet{KircherVotsmeier2025}: a conservative correction is physically useful only when positivity is
preserved at the same time.

Deployment followed a hierarchy. If \(c_{raw}\) satisfied \(Ac_{raw}=Ac_{in,*}\) and was nonnegative within
the numerical tolerance, it was returned directly. If the raw state failed but \(c_{aff}\) was nonnegative,
the affine state was returned. Otherwise, Equation~\eqref{eq:ch3_deployment_lp} returned the final deployed
state \(\hat{c}\). Reported composites were then computed as \(I_{\mathrm{comp}}\hat{c}\).

\subsection{Benchmark Models, Tuning, and Validation}

ICSOR was compared with nine unconstrained regressors: XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest,
support vector regression (SVR), \(k\)-nearest neighbors (k-NN), partial least squares (PLS), and a fully
connected multilayer perceptron (MLP). These comparators represented boosting, randomized trees, kernel
regression, instance-based prediction, latent-variable regression, and neural-network regression
\citep{Breiman2001,ChenGuestrin2016,Wold2001,Borisov2024}. The MLP used three hidden layers with widths 128,
64, and 32 and mapped the 22-dimensional input vector to the 20-component effluent state.

Hyperparameters for all retained models were selected by seeded Optuna studies with a tree-structured Parzen
estimator sampler, no pruning, and 100 trials per model \citep{Akiba2019}. Selection minimized validation
mean squared error in the shared 20-component effluent prediction space. The 8,000-row training pool from the
fixed benchmark split was reduced to a 4,000-row tuning subset, and that subset was split into 3,200
tuning-training rows and 800 tuning-validation rows. The selected settings were then fixed for the one-shot
benchmark and the repeated dataset-size analysis.

A row-wise 80--20 train-test split with seed 42 supplied the main benchmark, yielding 8,000 training rows and
2,000 test rows. The unconstrained regressors used z-score transformations fitted on the training split only,
separately for the 22-input matrix and 20-output component matrix, and their predictions were inverse
transformed before any physical diagnostic or composite metric was computed. ICSOR was kept in physical
coordinates so that the invariant operator, deployment rule, and composition map remained aligned with the
component basis. This preprocessing rule avoided leakage from the held-out test set \citep{Pinheiro2025}.

Sample efficiency and computational scaling were evaluated with a repeated size study. The retained totals
were
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_sample_sizes}
n\in\{500,1450,2400,3350,4300,5250,
6200,7150,8100,9050,10000\}.
\end{equation}
At each size, ten independent subsamples were drawn without replacement from the 10,000 accepted states using
seeds derived from root seed 42. Each subsample was split row-wise into 80\% training and 20\% held-out test
rows. The selected hyperparameters were not retuned during this sweep, so the resulting learning curves
isolated sample-size sensitivity rather than repeated tuning variability.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Interpretability Diagnostics}

Predictive accuracy was reported after every model's 20-component prediction was converted to COD, TN, TP,
and TSS through the common composition matrix. For a model \(m\), target composite \(k\), and held-out sample
set \(\mathcal{I}\), the composite residual was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_composite_residual}
e_{ik}^{(m)}=y_{ik}^{(m)}-y_{ik}^{\mathrm{ref}}.
\end{equation}
The aggregate and per-target summaries included root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE),
mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), mean squared error (MSE), and the coefficient of determination
\(R^2\). The fixed split used aggregate RMSE as the primary ordering metric because it measures error in the
same reported-composite space used for engineering summaries.

For the main accuracy comparison, ICSOR was evaluated at its final checked deployed output. The comparator
regressors were evaluated after the benchmark's common equality-only affine alignment and the same external
composition map. This alignment enforced the reporting invariants but did not impose componentwise
nonnegativity and was not the ICSOR linear program. Physical-admissibility diagnostics were reported
separately before composite collapse so that numerical alignment for accuracy would not be mistaken for a
learned guarantee of feasibility.

For a component prediction \(\tilde c_i\), mass-conservation and nonnegativity violations were quantified as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_physical_diagnostics}
v_{mc,i}=\|A(\tilde c_i-c_{in,i})\|_2,\qquad
v_{nn,i}=\|\min(\tilde c_i,\mathbf{0})\|_1,
\end{equation}
where the minimum is componentwise. A sample was counted as violating mass conservation or nonnegativity when
the corresponding quantity exceeded \(10^{-10}\). ICSOR diagnostics distinguished the raw coupled state,
the affine projected state, and the final checked deployed state. Comparator diagnostics used their direct
raw component predictions.

Learning behavior across sample sizes was summarized with final-size RMSE, normalized area under the RMSE
learning curve, dense average rank across retained targets and metrics, and the train-validation RMSE gap at
the largest retained size. The normalized area under the learning curve (nAUC) was computed by trapezoidal
integration over the sampled dataset sizes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_nauc}
\mathrm{nAUC}_{i,m}
=\frac{1}{n_{\max}-n_{\min}}
\int_{n_{\min}}^{n_{\max}}\overline{M}_{i,m}(n)\,dn,
\end{equation}
where \(\overline{M}_{i,m}(n)\) is the mean held-out value of metric \(m\) for model \(i\) at dataset size
\(n\). This summary makes the full learning curve visible rather than ranking models only at the largest
sample size \citep{VieringLoog2023}. Wall-clock training time was recorded for every repeated fit.

Interpretability was evaluated on the globally defined pre-deployment regression head rather than on the
piecewise correction returned by checked deployment. For reported composites, the raw-head coefficient map was
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch3_raw_head_coefficients}
B_{\mathrm{raw,comp}}=I_{\mathrm{comp}}\widehat{R}^{-1}\widehat{B}.
\end{equation}
The named blocks of this matrix were then inspected to identify operating effects, influent effects, curvature
within each input group, and interactions between operation and loading. The coupling matrix
\(\widehat{\Gamma}\) was inspected separately as the learned redistribution structure among activated sludge
components. Coefficient interpretation was therefore limited to structural patterns in the raw head and
coupling matrix; it was not treated as causal evidence about the biological process or as a local explanation
of the final deployed correction \citep{Rudin2019,Shen2023}.

\subsection{Fixed-Split Predictive Performance}

\section{Synthesis}

The results show why an integrated surrogate is a distinct contribution rather than a repetition of raw
prediction benchmarking or post prediction repair. The fixed-split accuracy results confirm that the most
flexible unconstrained learner, the MLP, gave the lowest reported-composite error. LightGBM also outperformed
ICSOR on aggregate RMSE. If the sole objective were minimum error in COD, TN, TP, and TSS, those models would
be preferable within the evaluated benchmark. ICSOR instead occupied a different position: it remained close
to the leading accuracy group while adding a deployment contract that the comparator regressors did not
provide.

The physical diagnostics are the strongest evidence for that distinction. Every unconstrained raw head
violated the stoichiometric conservation relation on every test sample, even when its reported-composite error
was low or its component predictions were nonnegative. This means that statistical accuracy and
component-space admissibility were empirically separate properties. ICSOR closed that gap only after checked
deployment: the raw regression head alone was not feasible, and the affine projection alone resolved only part
of the test set. The final deployment branch was therefore not an incidental correction but the mechanism that
converted a structured regression estimate into a usable activated sludge state.

The repeated size analysis adds a second qualification to the accuracy tradeoff. ICSOR was strongest at the
smallest retained dataset size and had the lowest RMSE nAUC across the full sample-size sweep. Its
train-validation gap was also small among the comparatively accurate models. These findings suggest that the
model's structural restrictions helped when data were limited and reduced train-test separation at the largest
size. The same restrictions also limited final-size accuracy relative to the MLP and LightGBM. The most
defensible interpretation is therefore a tradeoff among flexibility, sample efficiency, and physical
dependability rather than a universal ranking of model families.

The interpretability results further clarify the role of ICSOR. The model did not become interpretable because
every coefficient was sparse or causal. Instead, its raw head exposed named blocks for operating effects,
influent effects, within-group curvature, operation-loading interaction, and cross-component coupling. This
structure is more inspectable than an opaque direct map, but it still requires caution: the coefficients belong
to the pre-deployment head, and the final checked output is shaped by affine and linear-programming correction
when feasibility constraints are active. Interpretation should therefore focus on recurring structural
patterns rather than isolated coefficient magnitudes.

Within the stated steady-state reactor boundary, ICSOR provides a practical balance of accuracy,
data efficiency, physical admissibility, and structural inspection. Its evidence is limited to simulated
ASM2d-TSN component states within the sampled operating and influent ranges, and its conservation guarantee is
only as broad as the stoichiometric invariants used to define the feasible set. It does not establish field
validity, kinetic realism, or plant-wide conservation beyond the modeled boundary. The contribution is more
specific and more useful: it shows that an activated sludge surrogate can be designed so that the returned
component vector is not merely numerically plausible, but also exactly conservative, nonnegative, and
connected to an inspectable regression structure.

\section*{Summary of Contributions}

This research examined how machine learning surrogates of the activated sludge process can be developed and
evaluated so that their predictions are not only fast and accurate, but also physically admissible and
interpretable. Mechanistic activated sludge models represent biological wastewater treatment through coupled
component balances for organic carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, alkalinity, biomass, and solids, yet repeated
simulation of these models is costly when many operating and influent conditions must be explored
\citep{Henze2006,Guerrero2011}. Data driven surrogates promise to relieve that cost, but low prediction error
alone does not guarantee that a returned component vector can be read as a valid process state
\citep{Hansen2024,KircherVotsmeier2025}. The work addressed this concern through three connected studies that
treated predictive accuracy, mass conservation, nonnegativity, and structural transparency as joint
requirements rather than as separate goals.

The first study established a controlled surrogate benchmark for the complete effluent component state of a
steady-state Activated Sludge Model No.\ 2d with two-step nitrification (ASM2d-TSN) simulator. By comparing
thirteen tabular learners under shared sampling, folds, and scoring rules, it showed that the activated sludge
response is learnable from paired inputs and outputs, but that raw accuracy depends on the model family, the
amount of training data, the identity of the effluent component, and the degree of domain shift. Boosted trees
were the most data efficient at the smallest sample size, whereas a multilayer perceptron (MLP) used the
largest training set most effectively and achieved the lowest error and the highest component-level agreement.
The contribution of this study is a reproducible, component-space baseline that separates the learnability of
the mechanistic response from site-specific noise and narrow target definitions, and that documents which
components and operating regimes remain difficult even for the strongest raw surrogates \citep{VieringLoog2023}.

The second study showed that predictive accuracy and physical admissibility are empirically distinct
properties, and it provided a model-agnostic way to reconcile them. Every raw surrogate violated the
stoichiometric conservation relation implied by the adopted model boundary, and several otherwise accurate
surrogates also returned negative concentrations in low-value components. A positivity-preserving conservative
projection, applied after prediction and without retraining, returned every evaluated output to the declared
invariant balance and the nonnegative component domain, removing both failure modes across all in-distribution
and beyond-domain predictions with residuals at numerical precision \citep{KircherVotsmeier2025}. The study
also established that projection is not a device for improving ordinary error, because its effect on aggregate
accuracy was a measurable tradeoff that depended on the surrogate, the component, and the operating regime. The
contribution is therefore twofold, combining a dependable enforcement layer with a paired evaluation logic in
which admissibility diagnostics travel alongside accuracy metrics.

The third study integrated these requirements into a single surrogate design, Invariant-Constrained
Second-Order Regression (ICSOR). Rather than repairing an opaque prediction after the fact, ICSOR learns a
structured second-order regression head that keeps the engineering roles of operating variables, influent
loads, nonlinear curvature, and cross-component coupling explicit, and it returns predictions through a checked
deployment rule that guarantees exact conservation and nonnegativity. In the benchmark, ICSOR was the only
pipeline whose returned component states satisfied both physical conditions on every test case, while its
reported-composite accuracy remained within the leading group and only moderately above the best unconstrained
learners. It was additionally the most sample-efficient model at the smallest dataset size and had the most
favorable error profile across the full sample-size range, at the cost of higher fitting time and a dense but
block-structured coefficient map. The contribution is a demonstration that an activated sludge surrogate can be
constructed so that its output is simultaneously accurate, exactly admissible, and organized around an
inspectable structure, rather than achieving these properties only in isolation \citep{Rudin2019}.

Taken together, the three studies reframe what it means for an activated sludge surrogate to be credible. Speed
and low error are necessary but insufficient, because a usable surrogate should also return a component state
that respects the conservation and nonnegativity structure of the underlying model and, where possible, expose
the relationships it has learned \citep{Rudin2019,Shen2023}. The progression from raw benchmarking to post
prediction enforcement to an integrated interpretable design provides both a set of concrete results and a
transferable methodology. The same component-space evaluation, the same paired reporting of accuracy and
admissibility, and the same constraint-aware model construction can be applied to other process systems whose
surrogates must preserve conserved quantities, which positions the work as a contribution to physically
grounded surrogate modeling as well as to activated sludge simulation \citep{Karniadakis2021,Valente2025}.

\section*{Outlook}

The clearest direction for further work is validation beyond simulated data. The present evidence was obtained
under a known mechanistic reference, a fixed component basis, and a defined constraint structure, which made it
possible to isolate prediction error, conservation, and nonnegativity without confounding them with measurement
noise. Extending the framework to full-scale wastewater treatment records will require attention to sensor
error, calibration uncertainty, and unmodeled biological pathways, all of which complicate both the reference
state and the invariant structure used for enforcement \citep{Machado2014,Newhart2019}. A natural first step is
to test whether the admissibility guarantees remain meaningful when the conservation relation is estimated from
imperfect plant data rather than from an exact stoichiometric description.

A second direction is the move from steady-state prediction toward transient and dynamic operation. Activated
sludge plants experience diurnal and seasonal load variation, and many operational decisions depend on
time-dependent behavior rather than on a single steady state. Adapting the surrogate benchmark, the projection
step, and the checked deployment rule to dynamic trajectories would broaden their relevance to real-time
monitoring, setpoint design, and advanced process control \citep{Sadeghassadi2018,Croll2023}. This extension
raises open questions about how conservation and nonnegativity should be enforced along a trajectory rather than
at a single operating point.

A third direction concerns the scope of the physical guarantees themselves. The conservation used here reflects
the stoichiometric invariants of the adopted model within a declared reactor boundary, and it does not claim
elemental closure, thermodynamic feasibility, kinetic realism, or plant-wide balance. Later work could enrich
the feasible set with additional physically motivated constraints, couple the data driven surrogate with
differentiable mechanistic components, and quantify the uncertainty of both the prediction and the correction
so that admissible outputs also carry calibrated confidence \citep{Shen2023,Valente2025}. Strengthening the
guarantee in these ways would make admissible surrogates more defensible for detailed design and regulatory
reporting.

The methods can also be advanced on efficiency and scalability. The enforcement step and the ICSOR deployment
optimizer added modest but nonzero cost, and the integrated model required more fitting time than the fastest
baselines. Reducing simulation demand through active or sequential sampling, accelerating the constrained
deployment for higher-dimensional component sets, and exploring richer coupling and curvature representations
would improve the practicality of the approach for large screening and optimization workflows, where a
surrogate is queried many times \citep{BhosekarIerapetritou2018,Durkin2024,PadronPaez2020,Aboagye2021}.
Improvements of this kind would help the framework scale to plant-wide models with more components and
processes.

Finally, the results invite deeper integration with engineering decision-making. A surrogate that is fast,
physically admissible, and structurally inspectable is well suited to serve inside optimization and control
loops where each candidate solution must be both trustworthy and interpretable \citep{Rudin2019,Mao2024,Wang2025}.
Later studies could examine how admissible and interpretable surrogates change the quality and acceptability of
design and operating decisions relative to unconstrained black-box predictors, and how practitioners weigh a
small, well-understood accuracy cost against exact physical dependability and structural transparency. Answering
these questions would connect the methodological contributions of the research to the practical goal that
motivates them, which is more reliable and more explainable support for the operation of biological wastewater
treatment systems.

A single mechanistic benchmark supported all comparisons. Steady-state cases were generated from the ASM2d-TSN
simulator over a defined operating and influent domain using a seed-42 randomized Latin hypercube design
\citep{McKay1979}, and each case was accepted only when its steady-state balance residual met a fixed numerical
tolerance. Feature and target transformations were fitted on training partitions alone and inverted before
scoring, which prevented leakage from held-out data \citep{Pinheiro2025}. Hyperparameters were selected by
seeded Optuna studies with a tree-structured Parzen estimator \citep{Akiba2019}, and the selected settings were
held fixed across the nested and repeated sample-size analyses so that learning behavior reflected data quantity
rather than repeated retuning \citep{VieringLoog2023}. In-distribution performance was estimated by
cross-validation with shared folds, and beyond-domain performance used separately simulated excursions outside
the sampled envelope. This shared protocol makes the differences reported across the studies attributable to
model family and physical enforcement rather than to inconsistent data handling.


\chapter{Evaluation Metrics and Physical Diagnostics}
\label{ch:appendix.c}

Accuracy was reported in two spaces, and physical behavior was quantified through two diagnostics. Accuracy in
the component space used standardized errors so that components with different physical units could be combined,
whereas accuracy in the reported-composite space used errors on chemical oxygen demand, total nitrogen, total
phosphorus, and total suspended solids. Physical behavior was assessed before composite calculation so that
numerical accuracy would not conceal an inadmissible component vector. Table~\ref{tab:appC_metrics} summarizes
each quantity, the space in which it is defined, and what it measures.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Evaluation quantities used across the studies.}
\label{tab:appC_metrics}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
\begin{tabular}{p{4.6cm} l p{5.6cm}}
\toprule
Quantity & Space & What it measures \\
\midrule
Normalized mean squared, root mean squared, and mean absolute error & Component (standardized) & Average standardized component prediction error \\
Macro-average coefficient of determination & Component & Equal-weight variance explained across components \\
Root mean squared, mean absolute, mean absolute percentage, and mean squared error & Reported composite & Error on the COD, TN, TP, and TSS summaries \\
Coefficient of determination & Reported composite & Variance explained in the composite space \\
Mass-conservation residual & Component & Deviation from the stoichiometric invariants \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Component-space errors were normalized by the component standard deviations of the applicable training
partition, which prevents high-magnitude components from dominating an aggregate score and keeps the
in-distribution and beyond-domain results on a comparable scale. The macro-average coefficient of determination
weights every component equally rather than by variance, so that easily predicted components do not conceal
weak performance on more difficult ones. The mass-conservation residual measures the deviation of a prediction
from the sample-specific stoichiometric invariants of the adopted model boundary, and the nonnegativity
magnitude measures the total standardized negative mass in a prediction, so a component vector is physically
admissible in the sense used here only when both quantities lie within numerical tolerance. The normalized area
under the learning curve condenses an entire error-versus-sample-size trajectory into a single value, which
allows sample efficiency to be compared without ranking models at only one training size \citep{VieringLoog2023}.
